## Title

**Cybersecurity, Geotech Statecraft, and Switzerland’s National Cybersecurity Strategy**
Based on the talk/transcript by **Florian Schütz**, Director of the Swiss National Cyber Security Centre / Federal Office for Cybersecurity.

## Author / Speaker

**Florian Schütz**
**Role:** Director of the National Cyber Security Centre / Federal Office for Cybersecurity, Switzerland.
**Public mandate:** Coordinates implementation of the Swiss National Cyber Strategy and serves as a contact point for cantons, the economy, scientific communities, and the population.
**Prior appointment:** Appointed Federal Delegate for Cybersecurity by the Swiss Federal Council on **June 14, 2019**; responsible for establishing the National Cybersecurity Center and led it from **July 1, 2020** to the end of **2023**.
**Education:** Master’s degree in Computer Science; Master of Advanced Studies in Security Policy and Crisis Management from ETH Zurich.
**Experience:** More than ten years in cybersecurity.
**International role:** Since **January 1, 2022**, Chair of the OECD Working Party on Digital Security.

## Summary

The central argument is that the dominant “attack versus defense” framing of cybersecurity is technically and strategically insufficient. Schütz argues that cybersecurity should not be treated only as a defensive cost center, because attackers—especially criminal actors—also operate under economic constraints. A more advanced strategy should raise attacker costs, reduce profitable attack surfaces, and integrate cybersecurity into business resilience, supply-chain decisions, geopolitical strategy, and national sovereignty. He states that “95% of every attack is criminal in nature,” emphasizing that attackers make rational cost-benefit calculations. 

A major innovation in the talk is the reframing of cybersecurity as part of **geotech statecraft**. The speaker traces a historical shift from the 1990s internet optimism—where technology was viewed as a driver of democratization, peace, and commerce—toward today’s geopolitical interpretation of technology as a source of power, surveillance, military advantage, and strategic dependency. Technologies such as AI, 5G, semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, and data platforms are presented as dual-use assets that influence both economic growth and national security. 

The paper’s strongest technical-policy idea is that digital infrastructure must be understood as embedded in the physical world. Schütz highlights that AI requires massive power and cooling capacity, semiconductor production depends on highly concentrated global supply chains, and cloud/data-center strategy depends on electricity, land, logistics, and physical infrastructure. This challenges purely software-centered cybersecurity thinking and argues for a systems-engineering view of cyber risk. 

The talk also introduces a practical cybersecurity governance model for Switzerland built around three pillars: **cyber defense**, **cyber prosecution**, and **cybersecurity**. Cyber defense concerns wartime cyberspace and military systems; cyber prosecution addresses crimes and law enforcement; cybersecurity covers prevention, critical infrastructure protection, cyber threat intelligence, and cooperation with industry. The innovation here is organizational rather than purely technical: the state does not try to protect every organization directly, but instead enables organizations to protect themselves through intelligence, coordination, legal frameworks, and sector-specific collaboration. 

Schütz emphasizes that the National Cyber Security Centre’s mission is to make cyber threats understandable, provide tools to prevent attacks, limit incident damage, and increase the security of digital products and services. He explicitly argues that Switzerland’s role is not to replace corporate responsibility but to create conditions in which companies, critical infrastructure operators, and citizens can make informed and resilient decisions. 

A notable authorial idea is the critique of treating cybersecurity as “black magic” that executives and politicians can delegate to specialists. Schütz argues that technology is now core to business, cities, national infrastructure, and geopolitical competition, meaning board members, executives, and policymakers must understand technology at least strategically. This is presented as a leadership and governance challenge: cybersecurity can no longer be isolated as a narrow IT support function. 

## Key technical ideas

**1. Cybersecurity as economic pressure on attackers**
The author argues that defensive security is not enough. Effective cybersecurity should make attacks more expensive, less scalable, and less profitable. This is especially relevant for criminal ecosystems, where attackers optimize resources, targets, and expected returns.

**2. Actionable cyber threat intelligence**
A key technical distinction is made between interesting intelligence and actionable intelligence. Schütz gives an example where early knowledge of malware behavior allowed defenders to open firewall paths temporarily, preventing encryption and avoiding downtime before a patch was available. The innovation is the emphasis on fast, operationally usable CTI rather than raw indicators or abstract reports. 

**3. Cybersecurity as business resilience**
The author reframes risks such as unavailable updates, denied licenses, geopolitical export restrictions, and foreign technology dependency as business-continuity problems. The question is not only “Can we stop an attack?” but “Can we continue operating if technology access, support, updates, or supply chains are disrupted?”

**4. Data sovereignty and the panopticon effect**
The talk highlights the ability of platform operators or infrastructure controllers to derive strategic knowledge from data flows. This makes cloud location, data routing, platform dependency, and trust in infrastructure central cybersecurity questions—not merely procurement decisions.

**5. Sector-specific cyber centers**
Schütz promotes sector-specific cybersecurity centers for finance, healthcare, rail, and other industries. The technical rationale is that government alone lacks deep sector knowledge, while industry alone may lack broad threat visibility. Combining both improves threat interpretation and response relevance.

**6. Security of digital products and services**
The speaker argues that insecure digital products should be treated similarly to unsafe physical goods. His analogy is that toxic paint should not be sold in stores, and software or connected products full of vulnerabilities should also face stronger market or legal constraints. This supports product-security regulation, secure engineering, and cyber-resilience requirements.

**7. Engineering discipline in cybersecurity**
A recurring technical idea is that cybersecurity must return to engineering fundamentals: mathematics, system design, secure architecture, and measurable product quality. Schütz criticizes weakly engineered IT systems and argues that digital systems should be built with the same seriousness as physical infrastructure.

**8. Cybersecurity in geopolitical technology competition**
AI, semiconductors, 5G, cloud, and data are treated as strategic technologies. The paper links cybersecurity to export controls, investment restrictions, supply-chain regionalization, and competition between democratic and authoritarian systems.

**9. Public communication as cyber defense**
In the example of denial-of-service attacks during high-profile events, Schütz argues that technical mitigation alone is not always the decisive factor. Media literacy, public communication, and avoiding attacker message amplification can reduce the political and psychological impact of cyber incidents.

**10. National role: enable, not replace, private responsibility**
Switzerland’s model, as presented, is based on enabling organizations to protect themselves. The state provides threat intelligence, coordination platforms, incident support for critical infrastructure, and legal frameworks, but companies remain responsible for their own security investments and risk decisions.

## Overall assessment

The paper’s technical innovation lies less in proposing a new cryptographic protocol or security tool, and more in offering an integrated **cybersecurity systems model**. It connects technical security, economics, geopolitics, infrastructure engineering, supply chains, regulation, and organizational responsibility. The author’s main contribution is a strategic reframing: cybersecurity is not simply about stopping attacks, but about designing resilient digital societies where technology remains usable, trustworthy, economically viable, and politically sovereign.
