# Technical summary

This talk presents the U.S. Department of the Air Force Cyber Resiliency Office for Control Systems, referred to in the transcript as **Crocs / CROC / CROCS**, and its effort to build an **OT Cyber Defender training pipeline**. The core problem identified by the speaker is that modern military missions depend almost entirely on operational technology: electrical systems, water, gas, pumps, valves, breakers, and other infrastructure. According to the speaker, “about 100%” of missions rely on OT, and adversaries know this, so they are increasingly targeting infrastructure.

The central innovation is a workforce and training framework intended to create hybrid OT-cyber personnel who understand both engineering systems and cybersecurity. The speaker repeatedly describes these personnel as “unicorns”: people who can understand ladder logic, PLCs, RTUs, electrical grids, gas plants, pumps, valves, and physical process behavior, while also recognizing cyber indicators, incidents, and adversary activity.

The training model has two complementary directions:

1. **Teaching engineers cyber defense**
   - Engineers are treated as frontline defenders because they know the day-to-day behavior of the physical systems.
   - The goal is to move beyond ordinary annual cybersecurity awareness training and teach engineers to identify cyber incidents and indicators of compromise.
   - The described training track takes approximately **six to eighteen months**.
   - Engineers are trained to “engineer down” solutions, meaning to use physical or engineering constraints to reduce the impact of cyber compromise. The example given is a pump or valve that is physically limited so it cannot open beyond 75%, even if an attacker compromises a control system and commands it to open 100%.

2. **Teaching cyber defenders OT and engineering consequences**
   - Cyber defenders, including cyber protection teams, red teams, blue teams, active duty, guard, and private-sector teams, may be strong in cybersecurity but unfamiliar with PLCs and physical OT systems.
   - The speaker emphasizes that normal IT security actions can be dangerous in OT environments. For example, an active scan may overwhelm or break PLCs, take devices offline, or interfere with breakers and safety-critical operations.
   - Cyber defenders must learn how to respond to adversaries without jeopardizing the mission or causing physical consequences.

A key formal mechanism in the framework is **Work Role 462: Control Systems Security Specialist**. The speaker describes it as the flagship work role that CROC/CROCS and Mr. Daryl Hegley have been working to advance. It is not intended to stand alone, but to complement other workforce codes. For example, someone may already be a network specialist, and adding the control systems security specialist role would identify that person as a cyber defender focused on OT.

The speaker states that the team has worked with military stakeholders and industry partners, including the **DoD CIO workforce initiative**, to define what the training should look like and how it should meet Work Role 462. The role is aimed at the civilian and military workforce, and the speaker says they are also working toward including contractors, since contractors operate, maintain, and increasingly secure control systems on bases.

The initiative is intended not only for military use but also as a model for public-sector industry, utilities, and critical infrastructure. The speaker frames the effort as a transition from a military prototype to a broader public-good framework. He emphasizes collaboration with utilities and other critical infrastructure sectors, because defending systems “inside the gate” is insufficient if adversaries can compromise an external substation, pump, or other connected infrastructure.

The talk also mentions the use of **zero trust and AI**. The speaker acknowledges these as buzzwords but says they are relevant because mandates are emerging and adversaries are using AI. However, the transcript does not provide technical details about how zero trust or AI are implemented in the training pipeline.

A specific existing course is mentioned: **CS3300**, described as an introduction to combined OT and cyber training. Students get to interact with a PLC and “hack it,” in the speaker’s informal wording. More advanced courses are reportedly under development in collaboration with other units.

# Key technical ideas

## 1. OT cyber defense as a hybrid engineering-cyber discipline
The main technical idea is that protecting operational technology cannot be handled purely by traditional IT cybersecurity teams or purely by engineers. The authors’ / speaker’s proposed defender must understand:
- PLCs
- RTUs
- ladder logic
- pumps
- valves
- breakers
- electrical grids
- gas plants
- water systems
- mission impacts
- cyber indicators and incidents
- network behavior
- adversary activity

The innovation is not a single tool, but a structured workforce model that creates personnel capable of operating across both engineering and cyber domains.

## 2. Engineers as frontline cyber sensors
The speaker argues that engineers know the normal operating behavior of OT systems better than anyone else. Therefore, they should be trained to detect abnormalities that may indicate cyber events. This turns engineers into frontline defenders rather than treating them only as system operators or maintainers.

## 3. Cyber defenders must understand physical consequences
The talk stresses that standard cyber actions can create real-world safety and mission impacts in OT environments. For example:
- Quarantining a device could stop a pump.
- Active scanning could break or knock PLCs offline.
- A breaker failing to open during a catastrophe could cost lives.

The technical idea is that OT incident response must consider physical process behavior and mission impact, not just digital containment.

## 4. “Engineer down” as a cyber-resilience technique
A concrete technical concept presented is “engineering down” a solution. This means designing physical or engineering constraints so that a cyber compromise cannot drive equipment beyond safe limits. The example given is limiting a pump or valve to 75% open, so even if an attacker commands 100%, the physical system prevents the unsafe action.

## 5. Training environments modeled on real military base systems
The speaker says the training symbolizes or reflects systems found on military bases. The intended benefit is that responders do not spend the first 48 hours of an incident trying to understand what they are seeing. They should already know what PLCs, RTUs, and similar components look like and where to search for indicators of compromise.

## 6. Work Role 462: Control Systems Security Specialist
Work Role 462 is presented as the standardization mechanism for the training and workforce model. It is designed to identify and formalize personnel who specialize in control systems security. The speaker describes recent growth in units claiming Work Role 462 and interprets this as evidence that specialized OT-cyber personnel are needed.

## 7. Cross-training across OT, IT, cybersecurity, and policy
The proposed framework is multidisciplinary. The speaker says the workforce must know OT, IT, cybersecurity, policy, and other areas. This is presented as necessary for military installations, utilities, and broader critical infrastructure.

## 8. Public-good transition from military to industry and utilities
Although the framework originates in a Department of the Air Force context, the speaker’s goal is to make the methodology useful to industry, utilities, and public-sector critical infrastructure. The talk emphasizes collaboration, especially because military installations depend on infrastructure outside their own gates.

## 9. Inclusion of contractors in the workforce model
The speaker notes that many base control-system functions are performed by contractors, not only military or civilian personnel. Therefore, the framework is being expanded to account for contractors who operate, maintain, or secure control systems.

## 10. Zero trust and AI as future-enabling tools
The talk briefly identifies zero trust and AI as areas being leveraged, motivated by mandates and adversary use. The transcript does not provide implementation details, architectures, models, or technical mechanisms for either.
