## Technical summary

Thompson argues that cybersecurity controls designed for conventional IT and industrial control environments cannot simply be transferred into clinical settings. Clinical systems have different safety priorities: while availability matters, **integrity—the correctness and trustworthiness of clinical information—may create the greatest potential for catastrophic harm**. Availability failures are dangerous primarily because they delay care, and the consequences depend heavily on the device and clinical unit involved.

The presentation combines a detailed ransomware case study with findings from a study involving more than 300 clinicians. The study used interviews and surveys because hospital environments are too complex and institution-specific to simulate effectively. The research examines how cyber incidents affect patient care and how security controls interact with clinical workflows.

The case study concerns a 2019 ransomware incident at a hospital in Mobile, Alabama. The hospital shut down its network according to its business-continuity plan. Electronic health records were unavailable, and staff reverted to paper charting. In the labor-and-delivery unit, centralized fetal monitoring was also unavailable. Nurses were placed in patient rooms, but bedside alarms had previously been disabled to reduce alarm fatigue. Staff did not realize that these alarms needed to be re-enabled when the central monitoring system failed.

A patient undergoing an induction subsequently experienced fetal heart-rate decelerations that were visible on the physical monitoring trace but were not effectively communicated to the care team. The infant was delivered unresponsive and later died. Thompson presents this as a case in which ransomware was definitively linked to a patient death, while noting that litigation was ongoing.

The central lesson is that many safeguards functioned as intended: the hospital protected its electronic health records, the IT team followed its continuity plan, the fetal monitor continued to produce a trace, and a nurse was assigned to the room. However, the overall clinical process still failed because the system depended on unavailable communication and alarm functions, and because the cybersecurity incident was not adequately communicated to clinicians.

Thompson identifies several systemic weaknesses:

- Cybersecurity incidents were treated primarily as IT and data-protection events rather than patient-safety events.
- There was no effective communication channel between security personnel, clinical engineering, and frontline clinicians.
- The assumed duration of downtime was too short for the actual incident.
- Dependence on centralized monitoring was not understood until after the failure.
- Clinicians were not given meaningful information or choice about receiving care in a hospital experiencing ransomware.
- Business-continuity procedures did not fully account for clinical workflows and human behavior.

The study’s findings indicate that clinicians understand the need for cybersecurity and authentication, but they reject controls that obstruct urgent care. They prefer rapid mechanisms such as badge access and biometrics, particularly when working in protective equipment or under time pressure. The problem is therefore not simply clinician resistance to security; it is a mismatch between the design of controls and the conditions under which care is delivered.

The presentation gives several examples. Two-factor authentication at MRI workstations can be impractical because MRI rooms operate within Faraday cages. In one reported workaround, personnel authenticated outside the room and used tape to keep a mouse clicking. Endpoint security software can also create safety risks: the CrowdStrike outage reportedly caused medical devices to display failure screens or become unusable, requiring reboot procedures, manufacturer involvement, or replacement.

The infusion-pump example illustrates the same design failure from an operational perspective. A unit required one pump per bed, while biomedical engineering periodically collected unused pumps for patching. Because frontline staff lacked clear communication about the process, nurses hid pumps in cabinets to ensure that their unit would not violate its bed-to-pump policy. Thompson uses this behavior as evidence that clinicians will create workarounds when security or maintenance procedures conflict with operational requirements.

The authors’ broader proposal is to develop security controls around actual clinical processes rather than impose generic IT controls. Controls must preserve security while minimizing disruption to care, and security teams must work directly with clinicians, biomedical engineers, and device manufacturers. Thompson also proposes using the medical morbidity-and-mortality framework to analyze ransomware incidents, making cyber-related failures more transparent and connecting them to established patient-safety practices.

# Key technical ideas

1. **Treat cybersecurity as patient safety**  
   Cyber incidents should be evaluated by their effects on diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, and delays in care—not only by data loss or regulatory reporting.

2. **Prioritize integrity according to clinical risk**  
   The study suggests that manipulation of clinical information can create more severe harm than simple loss of availability. Clinicians may detect obvious anomalies through clinical judgment, but subtle or AI-assisted manipulation may be harder to recognize.

3. **Model availability failures as care-delivery delays**  
   Loss of availability is harmful because it removes information, alarms, or device functionality needed to make timely decisions. The impact varies by device type and clinical context.

4. **Design controls for real clinical environments**  
   Authentication and endpoint controls must account for conditions such as:
   - Full contact precautions and gloved hands.
   - Time-critical medication and treatment decisions.
   - MRI Faraday cages.
   - Long-lived medical devices running outdated operating systems.
   - The need for rapid access to patient information.

5. **Avoid blindly copying IT or ICS controls into healthcare**  
   Controls such as mandatory two-factor authentication or generic endpoint agents may be unsafe or unusable if they do not account for clinical workflows and device constraints.

6. **Recognize workarounds as design feedback**  
   Hiding infusion pumps or taping a mouse are not merely examples of poor security behavior. They indicate that the control or process conflicts with operational needs. Workarounds should reveal where security design must be changed.

7. **Integrate security, clinical engineering, and frontline communication**  
   A technically correct maintenance or incident-response process can fail if clinicians do not know what is happening or how their procedures must change during an outage.

8. **Use compensating controls for legacy devices**  
   Because medical devices have long product life cycles and may run obsolete operating systems, manufacturers and hospitals may need layered or compensating protections while upgrades are developed.

9. **Use empirical clinician research rather than unrealistic simulation**  
   The study relies on clinician interviews and surveys because hospital configurations and workflows are highly complex and difficult to reproduce in a laboratory or test environment.

10. **Apply morbidity-and-mortality analysis to cyber incidents**  
    The authors suggest adapting a clinical framework for examining adverse outcomes so that ransomware-related failures can be investigated with greater transparency and connected directly to patient-care consequences.
