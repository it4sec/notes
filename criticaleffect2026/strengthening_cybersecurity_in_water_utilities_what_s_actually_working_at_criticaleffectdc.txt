## Summary

This presentation describes the Cyber Readiness Institute’s approach to improving cybersecurity among small and medium-sized water utilities. The central argument is that practical cybersecurity adoption depends less on deploying sophisticated technology and more on helping organizations establish basic policies, assign responsibility, train employees, and receive trusted, hands-on guidance.

CRI’s program focuses on four foundational areas:

1. **Passwords**
2. **Software updates**
3. **Phishing**
4. **Secure storage and file sharing**

The program also requires organizations to prepare an incident-response plan and understand what to do when an incident occurs. The approach is based on the interaction of **people, processes, and technology**. The presenter emphasizes that technology alone cannot compensate for weak policies, untrained personnel, or unsafe user behavior.

The water-utility initiative began as a pilot in 2023, sponsored by Microsoft and supported by the Foundation for Defense of Democracies. According to the presentation, 89 utilities expressed interest, 59 started the program, and 35 completed it. The relatively low completion rate is presented as evidence that the primary barrier is not necessarily the absence of free resources, but a lack of trust, time, expertise, and confidence about where to begin.

The main intervention that distinguished this initiative from conventional online training was the addition of **Cyber Coaches**. Coaches complete CRI’s readiness and coaching programs and then help utilities interpret the material, prioritize actions, develop policies, and maintain accountability. They do not perform the work for the utility, but provide structure and guidance similar to a personal trainer.

The presenter argues that this coaching model made the program more usable and scalable than manuals or standalone online resources. Trusted partnerships with WaterISAC, AWWA, and NRWA helped CRI reach utilities that might otherwise have been reluctant to participate in a free external program.

A case study involving the **East Rio Hondo Water Supply Corporation** illustrates the intended impact. Before participating, the organization had some basic cybersecurity measures but lacked sufficient documentation and clarity regarding responsibilities, outsourced IT services, and incident handling. After completing the program, its administrator described the organization as moving from approximately 30% to 90% cyber readiness. The presentation stresses that this figure does not represent elimination of risk; rather, it reflects stronger preparedness and improved ability to recognize, prevent, and respond to threats.

CRI’s certification process requires:

- Policies aligned with CRI’s requirements
- Completion of a prioritization tool
- Completion of a software-update and asset-inventory tool
- An incident-response plan
- Training of employees
- Signed attestation by both the cyber leader and senior organizational leadership

CRI supplies templates and a playbook to reduce the burden on small organizations. The playbook is intended to remain a “living” document that is updated when personnel, systems, or technologies change. Because water utilities have operational-technology concerns that differ from those of ordinary small businesses, CRI also developed a water-utility-specific OT addendum with assistance from OT specialists.

The program is presented as a practical entry point rather than a complete cybersecurity framework. CRI positions it as a stepping stone toward more advanced standards and certifications, including NIST and ISO 27000, as well as a way to help organizations meet procurement requirements when working with larger partners.

## Key technical ideas

### 1. Cybersecurity readiness as an organizational system

The program treats cybersecurity as a combination of:

- **People:** Everyone understands their cybersecurity responsibilities.
- **Processes:** Policies, procedures, inventories, and response plans are documented and practiced.
- **Technology:** Technical controls support and enforce organizational policies.

This avoids treating technology procurement as a substitute for governance and user behavior.

### 2. Four-control baseline

CRI uses a deliberately limited baseline of four operational issues:

- Password security and, where possible, multifactor authentication
- Software updates and management of legacy systems
- Phishing awareness and prevention
- Secure storage and file sharing

The innovation is not the novelty of these controls individually, but their packaging into a manageable starting point for under-resourced utilities.

### 3. Compensating controls for legacy technology

When systems cannot be updated, the presentation recommends considering measures such as:

- Air-gapping
- Additional security controls
- Other safeguards that allow continued operation despite update limitations

The transcript does not specify particular products or configurations.

### 4. Coaching rather than self-service training

The Cyber Coach model adds human guidance to online materials. Coaches help utilities:

- Determine where to start
- Prioritize tasks
- Interpret requirements
- Develop policies and response plans
- Maintain progress and accountability

The presentation identifies trusted delivery and ongoing support as critical adoption mechanisms.

### 5. Verification of implementation

CRI’s verification process is intended to distinguish between merely claiming to have policies and actually implementing them. Verification includes confirmation that:

- Required policies exist
- Employees have been trained
- An incident-response plan is in place
- Leadership has formally attested to completion

### 6. Asset and software inventory as a readiness foundation

The prioritization and software-update tools require utilities to understand what they possess, including devices, laptops, servers, and software. This inventory supports decisions about updates, prioritization, and risk management.

### 7. Water-utility-specific OT adaptation

The program applies a general small-business cybersecurity model to water utilities while recognizing that operational technology creates additional requirements. The OT addendum is the mechanism used to adapt the playbook to water-sector conditions.

### 8. Incident response as part of basic readiness

The goal is not only to prevent attacks but also to ensure that utilities know how to respond and recover. Incident-response planning is therefore a required component of certification rather than an optional advanced capability.

### 9. Trust as a technical-adoption enabler

The presentation treats institutional trust as an important prerequisite for cybersecurity implementation. Partnerships with sector organizations helped establish that CRI’s free program and coaching did not conceal a commercial “hook.” This trusted distribution model increased access to smaller and rural utilities.

### 10. Foundations before advanced automation

One case illustrates a sequencing principle: organizations should establish basic policies, processes, and employee practices before pursuing advanced capabilities such as AI automation. The presenter’s position is that sophisticated tools are less useful when foundational controls remain undeveloped.

## Overall assessment

The presentation’s main technical contribution is an implementation model for cybersecurity readiness rather than a new security technology. It combines a small baseline of controls, structured templates and playbooks, leadership attestation, asset inventory, incident-response planning, and free human coaching. The authors’ central idea is that cybersecurity improvements in small water utilities are most likely to scale when they are practical, trusted, low-cost, and supported by a coach who helps organizations turn general guidance into completed operational actions.
