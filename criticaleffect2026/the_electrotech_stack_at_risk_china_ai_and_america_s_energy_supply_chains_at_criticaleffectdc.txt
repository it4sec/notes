## Summary

The presentation argues that the United States is entering an unusually rapid period of energy-infrastructure construction driven by AI data centers, electrification, new transmission, distributed energy resources, and digitally controlled grid equipment. This expansion creates both a security opportunity and a supply-chain vulnerability.

The authors use the term **“electrotech stack”** to describe the interconnected technologies that increasingly determine how electricity is generated, stored, controlled, distributed, and integrated with software. The stack includes relatively passive physical elements—such as battery materials and solar-panel components—as well as increasingly intelligent control layers, including inverters, battery-management systems, firmware, cloud integration, fleet-orchestration software, and advanced distribution-management systems.

Their central argument is that policymakers should not evaluate energy technologies only as complete products. Instead, they should decompose them into their underlying components and distinguish between:

- Physical or relatively passive components;
- Local control hardware;
- Firmware and software;
- Cloud-connected systems;
- Remote-update and fleet-orchestration capabilities.

This distinction is important because different parts of the same product present very different levels of strategic and cybersecurity risk. A battery’s electrochemical material, for example, does not pose the same immediate cyber risk as the software or cloud systems that manage a fleet of batteries.

The presentation identifies China’s major role in the electrotech supply chain as a source of several distinct risks. The authors emphasize that these risks should not be collapsed into a single category called “supply-chain security.” They distinguish:

1. **Economic dependency risk:** A disruption in trade could impair future deployments and replacement capacity, even if already-installed equipment continues operating.
2. **Immediate cybersecurity risk:** Compromise of connected control systems or fleet-orchestration software could affect grid balancing and operations.
3. **Firmware dependency risk:** Foreign vendors may retain access to deployed equipment through diagnostics, firmware updates, and maintenance channels, creating a potential avenue for later misuse.

The authors criticize blanket bans and broad “rip-and-replace” policies when they are applied without component-level analysis. In their view, such policies can fail to secure the most important control layers while slowing the infrastructure buildout and imposing replacement costs that governments, utilities, or consumers may not be able to fund. They use the Huawei and inverter examples to illustrate how restrictions may produce effects different from those intended.

Instead of a broad prohibition strategy, the presentation proposes the **“Moneyball” framework**, which ranks components according to urgency, technical risk, and strategic opportunity. The resulting tiers are intended to guide procurement, investment, restrictions, and domestic or allied production decisions.

The authors recommend that governments act quickly on the most consequential control layers while using the additional time to build domestic and allied capacity for other components. They also argue that industry—particularly hyperscale data-center operators—can help create market demand for secure components through procurement requirements and voluntary commitments, even where formal standards processes are too slow.

## Key technical ideas

### 1. Component-level analysis of the electrotech stack

The main technical innovation is the move from product-level evaluation to **subproduct and component-level analysis**. The authors argue that a product such as a battery, solar installation, or inverter should not be treated as a single undifferentiated security object.

The relevant question is instead:

> Which internal component or control layer creates the actual operational, cyber, or strategic risk?

This approach allows passive materials, local control systems, firmware, and cloud-connected orchestration systems to receive different treatment.

### 2. The “smart-to-dumb” spectrum

The authors describe components along a **smart-to-dumb spectrum**:

- **“Dumb” or passive layers:** Physical battery electrolyte, basic materials, and other components that primarily perform an electrochemical or physical function.
- **Intermediate layers:** Local hardware and control systems that may not communicate externally.
- **“Smart” layers:** Firmware, cloud integration, remote-update mechanisms, fleet orchestration, grid-management systems, and advanced distribution-management systems.

The farther a component sits toward the connected, software-controlled end of the spectrum, the greater its potential operational “blast radius” if compromised.

### 3. Three-way risk and opportunity assessment

The Moneyball framework evaluates each component using three questions:

1. **Deployment urgency:** How urgently is the component needed for AI infrastructure, grid modernization, or broader electrification?
2. **Technical risk and blast radius:** Where does the component sit in the smart-to-dumb hierarchy, and how much damage could compromise cause?
3. **Industrial opportunity:** Would U.S. or allied leadership in the component produce advantages beyond the energy sector, including benefits for defense, transportation, or communications?

The framework therefore treats industrial strategy as more than a risk-reduction exercise. It also asks where investment could generate broader national advantages.

### 4. Three sourcing and control tiers

The framework produces three broad procurement categories:

- **Tier 1 — Close domestic control and sourcing:** Components requiring the strongest domestic control because of their technical importance and potential consequences.
- **Tier 2 — Trusted allies and partners:** Components that can be sourced through coordinated allied supply chains.
- **Tier 3 — Managed global procurement:** Components for which greater sourcing flexibility is acceptable, provided that transparency and testing requirements are applied.

This tiered structure is intended to replace uniform restrictions with more selective controls.

### 5. Control layers as a cybersecurity “fire break”

A key systems idea is to use critical control layers as a **fire break** between lower-risk physical components and the operational systems that manage the grid.

The authors suggest that security policy should focus on ensuring that the most sensitive firmware, software, communications, and control functions are:

- Designed securely;
- Subject to appropriate sourcing requirements;
- Protected from unauthorized remote access;
- Governed by suitable testing and transparency mechanisms.

They also emphasize that geographic origin alone is insufficient: a component can remain insecure even if manufactured domestically if it is insecure by design.

### 6. Distinguishing economic disruption from cyber compromise

The presentation stresses that different risks have different timelines and consequences:

- A trade cutoff may mainly affect **future deployment and replacement**.
- A cyber compromise could have an **immediate operational effect**.
- Firmware access represents a continuing risk because vendor connectivity may persist long after equipment is sold and installed.

This distinction is intended to prevent policymakers from using a single remedy for fundamentally different problems.

### 7. Governance gaps for distributed and behind-the-meter devices

The authors argue that much of the new infrastructure is increasingly software-native, distributed, and connected behind the meter. Existing governance mechanisms—particularly the cited NERC CIP framework—were not designed for all of these newer device types and deployment models.

They recommend updating guidance so that distributed energy resources and behind-the-meter devices receive governance appropriate to their connectivity and control capabilities.

### 8. Hardware bills of materials

In response to a question, the presenters endorse the use of an **explainable hardware bill of materials**. They specify that it should go beyond the product’s commercial name and identify components at least several levels down.

This would support the Moneyball framework by allowing policymakers and operators to determine:

- What a product actually contains;
- Which components provide control functionality;
- Where foreign dependencies exist;
- Which layers require greater scrutiny.

### 9. Policy sequencing and industrial strategy

The authors recommend a staged approach:

- Secure the most urgent and consequential distributed energy resources now;
- Recalibrate foreign-entity and procurement rules around component-level tiers;
- Use targeted restrictions for specific high-risk use cases rather than broad product bans;
- Coordinate domestic and allied industrial strategies over the longer term.

The objective is to buy time: protect the most important control layers immediately while developing alternative sources for other parts of the supply chain.

### 10. Industry as a market signal

Because formal standards development may move more slowly than current infrastructure procurement, the authors propose that hyperscalers and other large buyers use procurement requirements to create demand for secure electrotech.

They point to voluntary commitments and power-purchase agreements as potential mechanisms for requiring greater scrutiny of critical control layers and encouraging adoption of agreed-upon security practices.

## Overall assessment

The presentation’s central contribution is a **selective, component-aware industrial-security framework**. Rather than treating every Chinese-made or foreign-supplied product as equally dangerous, the authors propose prioritizing components according to urgency, technical control, potential impact, and strategic value.

Their approach combines:

- Supply-chain risk analysis;
- Cybersecurity architecture;
- Industrial policy;
- Allied coordination;
- Procurement strategy;
- Hardware and software transparency.

The intended result is a more precise alternative to blanket bans: secure the control layers that can affect grid operations, preserve access to less sensitive commodities and components needed for rapid deployment, and invest strategically in technologies that provide advantages across energy, defense, transportation, and communications.
