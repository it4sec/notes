## Overview

Krapf presents a consequence-driven way of analyzing operational technology security: defenders should ask what would happen if an adversary understood their OT environment as well as—or better than—their own process engineers, PLC and SCADA programmers, and OT systems administrators.

The presentation uses a fictitious scenario to make the threat concrete. Rather than focusing primarily on conventional defensive controls, Krapf asks the audience to adopt an adversary’s perspective:

- Could a knowledgeable attacker remain undetected?
- Could defenders distinguish malicious activity from normal OT operations?
- Could the attacker manipulate physical processes without immediately disrupting the network?
- Would existing Prevention, Detection, Isolation, and Recovery capabilities limit the consequences?

The central argument is that **visibility is necessary but not sufficient**. Visibility should be treated as a gateway to security operations, not as a complete solution. Organizations may become complacent after deploying monitoring tools, assuming that the ability to see systems means they can understand, detect, and stop sophisticated activity.

Krapf connects attacker behavior to the knowledge held by OT personnel. Process engineers understand dependencies, interlocks, and operational tolerances; PLC and SCADA programmers understand control logic and configurations; and OT administrators understand networks, communications, servers, workstations, and firewalls. A capable adversary seeks to acquire the same knowledge through long-term reconnaissance, credential theft, supply-chain access, and “living off the land.”

The presentation emphasizes that OT attacks should be evaluated by their potential **physical and operational consequences**, not merely by whether an attacker can penetrate a network. Possible consequences discussed include mechanical damage, pressure or thermal events, chemical-process disruption, manipulation of safety systems, and gradual degradation that may not trigger conventional code-change detection.

Krapf concludes with a defense strategy based on understanding critical processes, controlling access by people and devices, detecting abnormal behavior, limiting third-party exposure, and applying cyber-informed engineering to reduce the physical effects of cyber manipulation.

# Key technical ideas

## 1. Think like an adversary with equivalent OT knowledge

The core analytical exercise is to assume that an adversary has detailed knowledge of the environment, including:

- Process dependencies and interlocks
- PLC and SCADA configurations
- Operator workflows
- Network and communications architecture
- Safety thresholds and tolerances
- Remote-access paths
- Critical equipment and process bottlenecks

This reframes security from “Can we detect an intrusion?” to “What could a knowledgeable intruder do while appearing to operate normally?”

## 2. Visibility is a gateway, not a solution

Krapf challenges the assumption that monitoring and asset visibility automatically resolve OT security problems. Visibility must be followed by:

- Interpretation of expected behavior
- Detection of abnormal access and device interactions
- Investigation of process anomalies
- Isolation and response capabilities
- Recovery planning

A system can provide visibility while still failing to identify a skilled attacker who uses legitimate tools, valid credentials, and normal administrative paths.

## 3. Adversary comprehension and prepositioning

Attackers may remain in an environment for extended periods to learn:

- How the facility operates
- Which safeguards exist
- Where set points can be changed
- What operational tolerances are acceptable
- Which systems are most consequential
- How to manipulate processes gradually without attracting attention

The purpose may be prepositioning: acquiring the knowledge and access needed to cause disruption later, even if no immediate destructive action occurs.

## 4. “Collapsing bag” approach to cyber-physical disruption

Krapf describes an adversary strategy that prioritizes physical end processes rather than immediately attacking network infrastructure. The attacker could:

1. Manipulate downstream physical processes.
2. Create increasing operational chaos.
3. Preserve the initial access path while manipulation continues.
4. Sever or remove the point of entry only at the last moment.

This approach seeks maximum kinetic impact while avoiding premature loss of control over the environment.

## 5. Physical consequences of OT manipulation

The presentation identifies several ways that control-system manipulation could damage equipment or disrupt operations:

- Repeatedly starting and stopping large motors or pumps, causing heat buildup and possible equipment failure
- Creating thermal or pressure spikes
- Producing water hammer through abrupt flow changes
- Altering chemical dosing in drinking-water or wastewater processes
- Manipulating generator behavior, as illustrated by the Aurora testing example
- Disrupting complex wastewater processes that depend on precise chemical control

The technical point is that cyber actions can be selected specifically for their mechanical, chemical, or process consequences.

## 6. Safety instrumented systems as high-consequence targets

Safety Instrumented Systems (SIS) are intended to protect sensitive processes when the primary control process fails. Krapf highlights the danger of compromising both:

- The main process-control system, and
- The SIS that should provide protection

An attacker who changes thresholds or bypasses safety logic may prevent the SIS from activating when required. The transcript cites the TRISIS malware as an example of targeting SIS environments.

## 7. Manipulating closed-loop control

Krapf discusses the risk of modifying closed-loop control behavior, including PID-based processes. An attacker might alter:

- Set points
- Feedback behavior
- Control parameters
- Sensor inputs

Such manipulation could cause a process to oscillate, drift, or behave abnormally without necessarily producing an obvious PLC-code change.

## 8. Sensor manipulation and data poisoning

Sensor integrity is presented as a distinct security issue. An attacker may manipulate sensor values or introduce gradual sensor drift so that the PLC receives misleading information.

This is important because:

- The PLC may execute its programmed logic correctly based on false inputs.
- Monitoring focused only on code changes may not detect the manipulation.
- Poor calibration practices can make it difficult to distinguish attack activity from ordinary sensor degradation.
- Verification of incoming data is therefore part of OT security, not merely maintenance.

## 9. Humans, contractors, and integrators as attack paths

The presentation identifies people and third parties as major risk factors:

- Shared credentials can enable unauthorized access.
- Contractors may retain unnecessary accounts or privileges.
- Systems integrators may connect to multiple utilities, creating concentrated supply-chain risk.
- A compromised integrator could expose several client utilities.
- Removable media and contractor equipment can undermine network separation.

Krapf describes Loudoun Water’s practice of not permitting integrators to connect their own equipment directly to its systems. Instead, the utility controls the equipment and observes the work performed on site.

## 10. Configuration remnants and temporary code

Temporary engineering changes can become persistent vulnerabilities. Examples include:

- PLC “forces” left in place after testing
- Temporary code that is not removed
- Forgotten configuration changes
- Unreviewed commissioning modifications

These remnants may be used by insiders or external attackers to produce unexpected process behavior, especially where OT monitoring is weak.

## 11. Publicly exposed engineering information

Krapf warns that publicly available technical documents can help adversaries understand a facility. Potentially useful information includes:

- Piping and instrumentation diagrams
- Asset schematics
- Engineering drawings
- Equipment specifications
- PLC and network requirements in public procurement documents

He recommends limiting access to such information through nondisclosure agreements or qualified-bidder processes rather than publishing detailed configurations openly.

## 12. Behavioral detection for people and devices

The proposed detection model extends beyond user behavior. Organizations should also understand device behavior, such as:

- Why a laptop begins communicating with a PLC for the first time
- Why a credential accesses a machine at an unusual hour
- Why a device communicates with systems outside its normal role
- Whether an account or device is performing actions inconsistent with its historical behavior

This is particularly important against “living off the land,” in which attackers attempt to resemble legitimate administrators and engineers.

## 13. AI as an attack accelerant

Krapf characterizes AI not necessarily as creating a fundamentally new vulnerability, but as making existing attack methods faster and more scalable. The transcript describes an incident involving jailbroken AI use, stolen credentials, and prompting techniques that reportedly compressed activity that might otherwise take days, weeks, or months into hours or minutes.

The resulting concern is a more “porous” attack surface: basic weaknesses such as exposed systems and unchanged default passwords become more dangerous because AI can accelerate reconnaissance and exploitation.

## 14. Crown-jewel analysis must include operations and supply chains

Krapf recommends analyzing crown jewels in two complementary ways:

1. **Critical operational processes:**  
   Determine the minimum processes required to continue operating, such as the minimum wastewater treatment functions needed to remain operational.

2. **Critical customers and suppliers:**  
   Identify important customers, chemical suppliers, and other dependencies whose loss could affect operations.

He stresses that this analysis cannot be limited to technical staff. It should include:

- Operations
- Logistics
- Maintenance
- Engineering
- Other personnel who understand process and supply dependencies

## 15. Layered controls and cyber-informed engineering

The defensive measures discussed include:

- Removing default passwords
- Removing systems from direct Internet exposure
- Micro-segmentation
- Deny-by-default access policies
- Zero-trust principles
- Data diodes where appropriate
- Next-generation firewalls
- Privilege cleansing
- Contractor-access management
- Vendor and equipment audits
- Out-of-band audits
- Penetration testing where feasible
- Sandboxing
- Behavioral analysis
- Cyber-informed engineering

Cyber-informed engineering is presented as a way to introduce physical or electromechanical controls that reduce the consequences of cyber manipulation. These measures may not eliminate every effect, but they can limit the severity of cyber-physical incidents, particularly for smaller utilities with limited cybersecurity resources.

## Overall contribution

The presentation’s main technical contribution is a **practical adversary-oriented model for OT risk analysis**. It links attacker knowledge directly to the expertise already present in OT organizations and then traces how that knowledge could be used to cause physical consequences while evading conventional detection.

Its most important ideas are:

- Treat visibility as an enabling capability rather than a completed defense.
- Analyze OT security through consequences and process tolerances.
- Monitor behavior involving people, credentials, and devices.
- Consider sensors, safety logic, temporary configurations, and supply chains as separate attack surfaces.
- Protect critical engineering information.
- Combine cybersecurity controls with physical and electromechanical safeguards.
- Use crown-jewel analysis to prioritize both essential processes and external dependencies.
- Support under-resourced utilities through mentorship, mutual aid, and practical assistance.
