## Summary

Oleg Shakirov argues that cyber-conflict analysis is too often dominated by catastrophic scenarios: a single, strategically timed attack that disables critical infrastructure, undermines military operations, or produces effects comparable to a “cyber Pearl Harbor.” Without dismissing the possibility of such events, he proposes shifting attention toward **“protracted disruption”**—the cumulative effects of numerous smaller, opportunistic, and sometimes geographically dispersed cyber incidents during a long-running conflict.

The Russia–Ukraine war is presented as a particularly useful case for examining this phenomenon. Shakirov characterizes it as the longest known cyber conflict, lasting more than four years—or more than twelve years depending on the selected starting point. He describes Russia and Ukraine as highly capable cyber powers with substantial expertise, and as relatively peer competitors in cyberspace. The conflict also involves non-state groups aligned with or tolerated by the states, increasing the volume and intensity of cyber activity.

According to the presentation, several highly anticipated catastrophic scenarios did not occur at the scale or concentration often predicted. The Viasat attack at the beginning of the war disrupted Ukrainian military communications, but did not eliminate them completely. Attacks on energy infrastructure were generally isolated to particular cities or regions and did not persist for long periods. However, the absence of a single catastrophic event did not mean that the cyber conflict lacked serious consequences. Instead, disruption appeared through repeated attacks against telecommunications, transportation, government systems, and supply chains.

Shakirov distinguishes the two forms of disruption:

- **Catastrophic disruption** is generally expected to occur at the onset of conflict, using access obtained and malware prepositioned in advance. It targets systems of high military or strategic importance and seeks to produce concentrated effects that create an immediate strategic advantage.
- **Protracted disruption** occurs throughout an extended conflict. Attackers continually probe systems, discover weaknesses, and exploit a broad range of targets. The objective is less about decisive advantage at one moment and more about attrition, imposing costs, and damaging the opponent over time. Its effects are diffuse, cumulative, and distributed across many organizations and individuals.

The presentation illustrates this model with examples. The 2023 attack on Ukrainian telecommunications provider Kyivstar disrupted voice and data services for customers across the country. The consequences extended beyond individual connectivity: ATMs, payment terminals, businesses, and some emergency services depended on the provider. In Russia, an attack on Orion Telecom disrupted internet access in four regions and took up to two weeks to resolve; the provider also served several closed military towns in Siberia.

Transportation incidents demonstrate how civilian systems can become strategically and socially important. A cyberattack on Ukrainian railways forced a shift from online sales to paper tickets, although trains reportedly continued operating. An attack on Russia’s Aeroflot caused approximately half of its flights to be cancelled on the day of the incident, although the airline restored its main flight operations the following day and used other airlines to transfer passengers.

Government platforms can serve as bottlenecks. Shakirov discusses attacks involving Ukraine’s ProZorro electronic procurement system and Russia’s Roseltorg procurement platform. The latter required roughly a week to restore core functionality and caused delays across multiple regions, including reported delays in medicine supplies. He also highlights systems whose effects extend across broader economic processes: an attack on a Russian alcohol-tracking system forced several beer factories to suspend operations because they could not ship products, while an attack on Russian customs systems forced border crossings to process declarations manually. Ukrainian state registers were also unavailable for weeks after a December 2024 attack, disrupting services that depended on them and imposing costs on the wider economy.

The supply-chain examples reinforce the argument that attackers do not need to destroy physical equipment directly. Disrupting one organization within a larger chain can delay production, transportation, or distribution elsewhere. Examples include an attack on Ukrainian agricultural company MHP that hindered product shipments, broader attacks affecting Ukraine’s agricultural and grain sector, and a ransomware attack on a relatively obscure Russian factory that supplied Rosatom and could potentially have caused delays across the wider supply chain.

Shakirov concludes that modern societies depend on densely interconnected systems with many potential points of disruption. During a prolonged conflict, the high volume of cyber activity makes some disruptive incidents almost inevitable, even if many attacks affect only individual organizations. Because the resulting damage is distributed, it is difficult to measure and may not attract the same attention as a spectacular outage. Nevertheless, the aggregate burden can be substantial.

The main resilience lesson is that defense cannot focus only on preventing compromise. Organizations must also be able to recover rapidly, maintain redundant ways of operating, and assist other organizations that depend on them. Shakirov recommends cross-sector coordination and information sharing, as no individual organization can be expected to defend itself successfully against every attack during a conflict. He also argues that avoiding or shortening cyber conflict, where possible, is preferable, since states that initiate conflict may also find themselves exposed to sustained retaliatory activity.

In response to a question, Shakirov acknowledges that cyber operations are an integrated part of modern warfare and that the examples can have serious effects, including consequences for public safety and critical services. For the United States, he warns that a future conflict involving China would almost inevitably have cyber implications extending beyond the most strategically important systems. He emphasizes that organizations may be less prepared than planners assume, making mitigation and recovery planning especially important.

## Key technical ideas

1. **Protracted disruption as a distinct analytical category**  
   The central innovation is the proposed distinction between catastrophic disruption and protracted disruption. The latter captures repeated, lower-scale incidents whose cumulative effects produce economic and social damage over an extended conflict.

2. **Shift from decisive effects to attritional effects**  
   Catastrophic operations seek a concentrated strategic advantage at a particular moment. Protracted disruption instead aims to impose continuing costs, consume recovery capacity, and weaken an adversary through attrition.

3. **Continuous access acquisition and opportunistic targeting**  
   Rather than relying exclusively on prepositioned malware in preselected strategic systems, attackers in a prolonged conflict may continually probe a broad range of systems, identify weaknesses, and exploit targets opportunistically.

4. **Diffuse and cumulative impact**  
   Damage may be spread across many organizations, regions, and individuals. This makes the aggregate effect difficult to assess, even when individual incidents appear limited.

5. **Civilian systems as sources of strategic and societal disruption**  
   Telecommunications, civilian transportation, payment infrastructure, emergency services, government platforms, and commercial supply chains can be as consequential to societal resilience as explicitly military systems.

6. **Dependency and bottleneck analysis**  
   A system does not need to be physically destructive or directly connected to critical infrastructure to have broad effects. Centralized procurement systems, customs platforms, product-tracking systems, and state registers can act as bottlenecks within larger economic and administrative processes.

7. **Indirect disruption through interdependent systems**  
   Attackers can cause disruption by targeting a dependency within a supply chain rather than the final production facility or physical equipment. The effects can propagate through logistics, distribution, procurement, and service delivery.

8. **Resilience as recovery capability, not only prevention**  
   In a high-volume cyber conflict, successful prevention of every attack is unrealistic. Resilience therefore depends on restoration speed, operational continuity, redundancy, and the ability to revert to manual or alternative processes.

9. **Redundancy and practiced fallback operations**  
   Examples such as paper-based railway ticketing and manual customs processing demonstrate the value of alternate procedures. Shakirov’s recommendation is to establish and practice these fallback mechanisms before disruption occurs.

10. **Collective defense across organizational boundaries**  
    Because interconnected systems transmit effects across organizations, resilience requires coordination, information sharing, and recovery planning that extends beyond an individual organization’s own network perimeter.

11. **Strategic planning under conditions of limited preparedness**  
    Policymakers should not assume that organizations will be fully prepared for wartime cyber activity. Planning should address how to mitigate and recover when attacks succeed, rather than relying solely on preventive controls.

## Overall assessment

The presentation’s main technical and conceptual contribution is a reframing of cyber-conflict risk. Instead of treating disruption primarily as a single catastrophic event against a high-value target, it presents cyber conflict as a potentially sustained process of probing, exploitation, service interruption, and cumulative economic and social damage. The framework directs defensive attention toward dependencies, bottlenecks, fallback operations, recovery speed, and cross-organizational resilience.
