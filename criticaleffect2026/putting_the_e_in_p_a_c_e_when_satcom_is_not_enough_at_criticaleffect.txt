What happens to your situational awareness when both the grid and your networks go down and stay down for weeks? ## Summary

The talk presents **Flare**, a MITRE research-and-development project intended to preserve basic critical-infrastructure status reporting and coordination during prolonged, large-scale communications and power outages. The project addresses a gap exposed by a MITRE exercise: there is currently no national Common Operating Picture (COP) for critical infrastructure when conventional communications—including IP-based networks, cellular, satellite, and landline systems—are unavailable for extended periods.

The motivating problem is not merely loss of voice or data communications. According to Bristow, when organizations cannot communicate, they cannot coordinate restoration activities, prioritize scarce resources, or maintain a shared understanding of infrastructure conditions. This can prevent recovery of mutually dependent systems, such as the power grid and communications infrastructure, creating what he describes as interlocking dependencies that can cause cascading failure.

Flare was developed in response to findings from a late-2024 classified exercise involving approximately 70 asset owners and operators. The exercise modeled a Taiwan Strait conflict scenario and continued through 45 days after the start of hostilities. It examined not only initial disruption but also the neglected recovery phase. Participants encountered localized decision-making, conflicting operational directives, inadequate long-duration continuity plans, uncertainty over infrastructure status, and difficulty restoring the power system without reliable communications.

The proposed solution is a **low-cost, multimodal communications and status-reporting system**. Flare devices provide simple operational states—such as whether a node is alive, dead, partially functional, or missing a resource needed for recovery—and transmit those states over resilient communications paths. The system is designed to function with minimal infrastructure and limited bandwidth rather than to replace ordinary high-bandwidth communications.

The presentation reports that a later MITRE simulation, conducted with participation from PJM, used Flare-generated information to support simulated systemic power restoration. The exercise supplied generation, transmission, and load status information sufficient to identify black-start cranking paths and restore the simulated power system. Bristow presents this as evidence that relatively limited status information can support consequential restoration decisions.

Flare was still at the prototype stage at the time of the talk. Ten devices existed, with prototypes targeting a price of approximately **$500 per unit**. MITRE was working with private-sector companies to productize the design and was preparing field tests with a utility.

## Technical innovation and authors’ ideas

The authors’ central technical idea is to design communications for the **emergency phase of the P.A.C.E. framework**, where primary, alternate, and contingency systems have failed or become untrustworthy. Instead of assuming that satellite or internet connectivity will remain available, Flare is designed around independent, low-bandwidth, infrastructure-light communication.

A second major idea is to reverse the information-flow model used in nuclear command and control. Bristow explains that nuclear command and control emphasizes the delivery of commands from a central authority. Flare instead emphasizes the collection and aggregation of many infrastructure-status reports so that decision-makers can make coordinated restoration decisions.

The system combines:

- **Low-bandwidth operational status messages** rather than voice, video, or large data transfers.
- **Multimodal communications**, using the internet when available, and RF or satellite communications when appropriate.
- **RF as a foundational mode**, because it can operate without dependence on the conventional communications core.
- **Mesh networking**, allowing devices to discover available communication paths and route messages around local interference or outages.
- **LoRa for localized communication**, described as supporting distances of approximately six miles.
- **HF for longer-distance links**, including mechanisms for operating with a limited number of long-haul connections.
- **Solar and battery power**, with a stated battery life of at least 30 days and the possibility of substantially longer operation with solar support.
- **Local and hierarchical aggregation**, allowing status information to move from infrastructure nodes to local control centers and, where desired, to state, local, and federal decision-makers.
- **Tiered cryptography**, intended to allow organizations to control how much information is shared with higher-level authorities.
- **A Common Operating Picture**, presented through a laptop-based interface and intended eventually to use ArcGIS through collaboration with Esri.

## Key technical ideas

1. **Design for communications collapse, not just degradation**  
   Flare targets conditions in which internet, cellular, satellite, and other conventional systems may be unavailable for weeks or may require complete reconstruction.

2. **Minimal status information can enable major decisions**  
   Messages such as “alive,” “dead,” “partially operational,” or “requires resource X” may be sufficient to support infrastructure prioritization and power-system restoration.

3. **Resilient coordination requires a network of nodes**  
   The system uses distributed devices, mesh networking, and local aggregation instead of relying on one centralized communications path.

4. **RF independence is a core resilience mechanism**  
   RF is used because it can operate without the conventional telecommunications core and can be powered by batteries or solar panels.

5. **Multimodal communications provide graceful fallback**  
   Flare can use the internet when available, but can switch to RF or satellite where those options remain viable. The design does not assume that any single communications technology will always work.

6. **Long-range coordination requires HF and constrained-link management**  
   For geographically distributed restoration, the system combines local links with HF long-haul paths and attempts to manage limited bandwidth by restricting traffic to essential information.

7. **The COP is both a technical and organizational capability**  
   The system is intended to allow infrastructure owners and government authorities to build a shared status picture while preserving different levels of information access.

8. **Recovery must be planned as a distinct operational phase**  
   The authors emphasize that exercises often stop when the crisis peaks. Flare is motivated by the need to coordinate recovery over weeks or months after the initial disruption.

9. **The system is intended for operational scale**  
   Initial estimates expanded from perhaps 1,000–2,000 devices to requirements of roughly 5,000 for PJM and approximately 10,000 for one large utility, motivating private-sector manufacturing and product development.

10. **Prototype validation is moving toward field testing**  
    The project had completed bench testing and a Massachusetts-to-Washington test, with a utility field test planned. Full nationwide testing had not yet been completed.

## Overall assessment based on the source

The talk’s innovation lies less in creating a high-capacity communications system than in combining **simple infrastructure telemetry, resilient low-bandwidth networking, distributed status aggregation, and hierarchical decision support** for a prolonged “dark sky” scenario. The authors argue that restoration may depend on knowing a small number of critical facts across many infrastructure nodes, even when normal communications and centralized coordination are unavailable.
