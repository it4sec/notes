## Summary

The presentation examines how emerging markets are developing OT cybersecurity policies and why these approaches matter to technology providers, infrastructure operators, policymakers, and US competitiveness and security.

Casserly argues that emerging markets face many of the same OT cybersecurity pressures as advanced economies, but that the **scale, pace, and structure** of these pressures are often different. Emerging markets frequently experience rapid infrastructure growth, a larger proportion of greenfield projects, substantial urbanization, expanding digital energy systems, and constrained cybersecurity budgets and skills. These conditions can create both serious risks and opportunities to incorporate security into new infrastructure rather than retrofit legacy systems.

The presentation identifies several forces shaping OT security policy:

- **Supply-chain diversification:** Countries such as India, Vietnam, and Mexico are receiving significant manufacturing and supply-chain investment.
- **Urbanization and smart-city development:** Governments are deploying digital infrastructure to support urban growth. Examples include Dubai’s replacement of conventional water meters with smart meters and India’s objective of replacing 250 million conventional electricity meters with smart meters.
- **Energy expansion and grid modernization:** Emerging markets are adding software-defined energy infrastructure, renewable generation, and distributed energy resources. India is presented as a major example, with rapidly increasing electricity consumption and substantial planned non-fossil generation.
- **AI and data-center investment:** India, Thailand, Malaysia, and Brazil were identified as major recipients of data-center investment.
- **Resource constraints:** OT cybersecurity skills and budgets are limited, especially where cybersecurity competes with other development priorities.
- **Geopolitical and sovereignty concerns:** Emerging markets often seek cooperation with multiple major powers while preserving national sovereignty. These concerns influence technology procurement and policymaking.

## Analytical framework

Casserly proposes a four-part framework for evaluating OT cybersecurity policy in emerging markets:

1. **Ease of compliance**
   - Whether a regulatory framework exists.
   - How prescriptive it is.
   - How seriously it is enforced.
   - The time, effort, and cost needed to comply.

2. **Standards acceptance**
   - The degree to which policies align with internationally accepted technical standards.
   - Particular attention is given to the ISA/IEC 62443 family of standards for industrial automation and control-system cybersecurity.

3. **Accessibility to regulators**
   - Whether companies and other stakeholders can reach relevant authorities and participate in policymaking.

4. **Engagement style**
   - How regulators communicate, interpret requirements, and involve private-sector participants.

The framework therefore evaluates policy not only as a compliance burden, but also as an environment in which companies and policymakers can shape future requirements.

## Country case studies

### Brazil

Brazil is characterized as having a **low compliance burden and high dialogue value**.

The presentation emphasizes Brazil’s preference for open, transparent policymaking and public-private partnerships. A draft national cybersecurity law reportedly calls for the celebration of public-private partnerships, reflecting the country’s intended approach to cyber governance.

Brazil’s developing digital relationship with the European Union is also highlighted. A data-adequacy agreement and a digital partnership agreement may increase the possibility that elements associated with the EU Cyber Resilience Act will influence future Brazilian regulations. Casserly presents this as an issue for practitioners to monitor and engage with, rather than as an established outcome.

A distinctive feature of Brazil and Latin America more broadly is the role of **development banks**. These institutions are important participants in cybersecurity policy discussions and can serve as channels of influence and engagement that are less prominent in advanced-market policy processes.

Brazil is described as representative of a broader Latin American environment in which OT cybersecurity policy remains relatively **nascent**, creating an opportunity for early engagement.

The presentation also briefly discusses Chile, which adopted a national critical-infrastructure cybersecurity framework in 2024 and selected NERC CIP as a model for electricity-sector cybersecurity. Casserly questions whether that model is well suited to newer distributed and software-defined energy systems.

### Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia is presented as having one of the most comprehensive OT cybersecurity regulatory structures among the markets discussed.

Its approach is linked to **Vision 2030**, the country’s plan to diversify and modernize its economy beyond oil. Cybersecurity and domestic cyber capacity are treated as strategic enablers of this transformation. At the same time, the policy environment is highly centralized under the National Cybersecurity Authority.

The regulatory model begins with broadly applicable essential cybersecurity controls, supplemented by additional control sets for areas such as:

- Cloud cybersecurity
- Data cybersecurity
- Critical systems
- Operational technology

ISA/IEC 62443 is described as covering much of the technical distance toward compliance with Saudi OT controls for vendors, system integrators, and asset owners/operators. However, additional requirements extend beyond the technical standard, including requirements related to the employment of Saudi nationals in cybersecurity roles.

The managed-service-provider regime illustrates Saudi Arabia’s emphasis on national control. Licensing requirements reportedly include localization of:

- Data
- Ownership and headquarters
- Workforce
- Security operations center personnel

Licensed providers must also give the Saudi government continuous access to their security operations center. The presentation describes these requirements as highly restrictive, with only four providers having obtained the relevant license at the time discussed.

Saudi Aramco is identified as another important policy actor because of its economic significance. In addition to national requirements, it applies its own controls to vendors, system integrators, components, and systems. These controls are described as largely aligned with ISA/IEC 62443.

### India

India is characterized by **fragmented authorities, complex compliance, and relationship-dependent engagement**.

Unlike Saudi Arabia’s centralized and prescriptive model, India’s cybersecurity responsibilities are distributed across multiple agencies and regulatory bodies. This creates uncertainty about:

- Which requirements are mandatory
- How different guidelines and regulations interact
- Which authority should be approached
- How individual agencies interpret particular terms

Casserly presents direct relationships with officials and agencies as particularly important for understanding the practical meaning of requirements.

A central technical example is India’s pending power-sector cyber regulation, developed by the Central Electricity Authority. The proposal is described as addressing the cybersecurity challenges of a more distributed and software-defined energy system. It includes obligations for OT vendors, system integrators, and asset owners/operators.

The proposal also contains notable **bill-of-materials requirements and guidance**, extending beyond conventional software bills of materials. The material discussed includes:

- Software bills of materials
- Hardware bills of materials
- Cryptographic- and quantum-readiness bills of materials
- AI bills of materials

Casserly does not present these provisions as fully mature or necessarily workable. Instead, she identifies them as useful material for examining how future asset, component, software, AI, and cryptographic dependencies might be represented in cybersecurity governance.

### Indonesia

Indonesia is characterized as **ambiguous**.

The country has critical-infrastructure cybersecurity requirements that reportedly mandate minimum security controls for operators and 24-hour incident reporting to sector-specific computer emergency response teams. However, the presentation states that there was no evidence that the required minimum controls or sector-specific teams had actually been published or established. Consequently, operators and suppliers may have difficulty determining what compliance requires in practice.

Indonesia’s pending cybersecurity and resilience legislation is technically significant because it introduces obligations for manufacturers of products with digital elements. Casserly identifies this as a direct example of language and concepts associated with the EU Cyber Resilience Act appearing in proposed legislation outside Europe.

She describes Indonesia as the only case studied where she observed a relatively literal copy of EU Cyber Resilience Act language. This is contrasted with Brazil, where possible European influence appears more likely to result from ongoing cooperation and a shared interest in product security than from direct copying.

Indonesia’s geography, linguistic diversity, and capacity constraints also make consistent enforcement difficult.

## Technical innovation and authors’ ideas

The main contribution is not a new security protocol or detection technology. Instead, the presentation offers a **policy-analysis method for understanding OT security in emerging markets** and draws attention to technical and governance mechanisms that may become increasingly important.

Its most important ideas are:

1. **Treat emerging markets as distinct policy environments**
   - The authors reject a one-size-fits-all compliance or engagement strategy.
   - Emerging markets should not be assumed to be copying US or European approaches in the same way or for the same reasons.

2. **Use ISA/IEC 62443 as a cross-market technical baseline**
   - The standard is presented as relevant across vendors, system integrators, and asset owners/operators.
   - Its role varies by country: it is closely related to Saudi requirements, useful in India, and potentially relevant to emerging product-security regimes in Brazil and Indonesia.

3. **Evaluate policy through both compliance and influence**
   - A framework can be technically sound but difficult to interpret or engage with.
   - Accessibility to regulators and engagement style are therefore treated as important policy dimensions alongside formal requirements.

4. **Recognize the security implications of greenfield infrastructure**
   - Rapid deployment of new energy, smart-city, manufacturing, data-center, and AI infrastructure creates an opportunity to build in OT security early.
   - This differs from approaches focused primarily on securing legacy infrastructure.

5. **Expand bills of materials beyond software**
   - India’s proposed approach is notable for considering hardware, cryptographic and quantum readiness, and AI-related information.
   - Even if parts of the guidance are difficult to implement, it provides a basis for thinking about broader system dependencies and future security requirements.

6. **Link product security to infrastructure security**
   - Brazil and Indonesia illustrate the emergence of requirements for products with digital elements.
   - The presentation connects the security of products and components to the resilience of the infrastructure in which they are deployed.

7. **Include non-state and non-national actors in the policy analysis**
   - Large companies such as Saudi Aramco and multilateral development banks can influence OT security requirements and investment decisions.
   - National regulators are therefore not the only relevant policy actors.

8. **Use commercial diplomacy and development finance as security levers**
   - The presentation recommends incorporating OT security into US trade, commercial diplomacy, procurement, capacity-building, and development-bank activities.
   - This is especially relevant to new AI and energy infrastructure, where OT systems provide the underlying operational foundation.

## Key technical ideas

- **ISA/IEC 62443 as a portable baseline:** A common technical reference can support engagement across diverse national regimes, although local legal, workforce, localization, and sovereignty requirements may extend beyond it.
- **Security for software-defined and distributed energy:** Emerging energy systems require governance models that account for distributed energy resources, renewable generation, software-defined control, and changing ownership structures.
- **Broader bills of materials:** Security inventories may need to cover software, hardware, cryptographic posture, quantum readiness, and AI components or dependencies.
- **Product security for digitally enabled devices:** Requirements for manufacturers of products with digital elements can connect supplier obligations to infrastructure cybersecurity.
- **Compliance interpretability as a technical-operational issue:** Ambiguous or fragmented regulations can make it difficult for operators and suppliers to determine which controls, reporting duties, and assurance activities are actually required.
- **Localization as part of OT security governance:** In Saudi Arabia, data location, ownership, workforce composition, and government access to security operations are integrated into the cybersecurity regime.
- **Greenfield security opportunities:** New infrastructure investment offers opportunities to embed OT security requirements during design, procurement, and deployment rather than relying only on retrofitted controls.
- **Multi-actor governance:** Development banks, major infrastructure companies, system integrators, vendors, and national regulators all influence the practical security baseline.

## Overall assessment

The presentation’s central technical-policy insight is that OT security in emerging markets cannot be understood solely through the presence or absence of national regulations. Effective analysis must also consider infrastructure growth patterns, standards alignment, institutional fragmentation, enforcement capacity, regulator accessibility, development finance, corporate influence, and national sovereignty objectives.

Casserly’s recommended approach is to engage early, use ISA/IEC 62443 where applicable as a common technical foundation, examine emerging approaches such as expanded bills of materials, and tailor compliance and policy-engagement strategies to each market rather than exporting a single US or European model.
