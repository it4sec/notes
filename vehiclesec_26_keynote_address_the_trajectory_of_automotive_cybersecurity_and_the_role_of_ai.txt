Keynote Address: The Trajectory of Automotive Cybersecurity and the Role of AI

André Weimerskirch, Block Harbor Cybersecurity

Automotive cybersecurity has evolved through distinct phases, moving from limited industry awareness and a period of hype to today's regulated landscape. Despite substantial technological changes in vehicle architectures and connectivity over the last decade, confirmed security incidents remain largely constrained to vehicle theft and chip tuning. As the industry matures, investment patterns are shifting toward value-based decision making to meet regulatory compliance targets. The emergence of AI introduces a critical shift. On the threat side, AI may lower the technical and economic threshold for attacks, expanding the pool of capable adversaries and targeting previously unattractive systems. Conversely, AI offers immense potential for defense: it can accelerate vulnerability reduction, enable faster attack detection at scale, and automate security tasks to help stakeholders meet compliance goals. Crucially, the introduction of AI also creates new operational challenges, such as the need to automatically update vehicle firewalls within minutes and compress software update deployment timelines from months to hours. This talk provides a structured analysis of the field's historical trajectory, its current posture, and the specific security gaps that AI can and cannot close.

## Technical summary

The keynote presents automotive cybersecurity as having moved through several stages: initial low awareness, a period of intense public attention and hype, and the current phase dominated by regulation and compliance. Weimerskirch argues that the industry has made substantial progress in implementing baseline vehicle protections, but that its processes remain expensive, manual, and difficult to scale.

The central technical argument is that the next major challenge is not simply designing better security mechanisms. It is operating automotive cybersecurity efficiently across complex vehicle platforms, suppliers, regulations, software updates, and long product lifecycles. AI is presented as a potential enabler for this transition, but also as a tool that can reduce the cost and expertise required for attacks.

The speaker distinguishes between attacks that are technically possible and attacks that are economically attractive. To date, confirmed automotive security incidents are described as being concentrated mainly in **vehicle theft and chip tuning**, where attackers have clear financial incentives. By contrast, many technically impressive attacks have not become widespread because they do not scale, are difficult to monetize, or expose attackers to significant legal risks.

Automakers primarily focus on attacks that can affect large numbers of vehicles, especially attacks through wireless interfaces. The standard defensive architecture described in the talk includes:

- Secure in-vehicle communication.
- Segmentation between connectivity systems and critical embedded systems.
- Secure software updates and code signing.
- Secure boot.
- Secure diagnostics.

These controls are considered broadly standard in modern vehicles. However, the compliance process surrounding them is highly labor-intensive. Risk assessments may take weeks per module, audits add substantial overhead, and the work must be repeated across software updates, hardware changes, vehicle platforms, and multiple regulatory frameworks.

Weimerskirch argues that compliance has become the practical target because it is measurable and legally required, while security becomes a byproduct. He proposes reversing this perspective: if organizations treat security as the primary objective and build efficient processes around it, regulatory compliance can become the byproduct.

The keynote also examines future shifts in the automotive threat landscape. Nation-state attacks could become more relevant because connected vehicles and over-the-air update capabilities could theoretically be used to disable large fleets. Software-defined vehicles may consolidate many electronic control units into a small number of powerful computing platforms, potentially enabling stronger security mechanisms but also creating new concentration risks. Autonomous-vehicle fleets may have a different security profile because they require persistent connectivity, remote assistance, and are operated by comparatively wealthy companies that would suffer substantial losses from downtime.

AI is described as having both offensive and defensive effects. On the offensive side, AI may help attackers reverse-engineer firmware and identify vulnerabilities after they have obtained firmware through a physical attack. The speaker gives the example of an MCP server for Infineon TriCore firmware analysis. AI does not remove the need for physical access, but it can accelerate the analysis phase and lower the cost of attacks. This may make previously unattractive targets economically viable.

On the defensive side, AI could support automated vulnerability discovery and patching, vehicle security operations centers, anomaly detection, risk analysis, and security-product development. The speaker cites results from DARPA’s AI Cyber Challenge, including figures of vulnerabilities being found and patched in substantially less time and at much lower cost than through conventional manual processes.

A major operational goal is to reduce the time required to respond to newly discovered vulnerabilities. Although modifying software may take only a day, coordinating automakers, suppliers, purchasing, validation, testing, and deployment can extend remediation to approximately six months. Weimerskirch argues that AI-assisted process management could reduce this timeline to hours. Similarly, vehicle firewall rules that currently take considerable effort to update should be changed automatically within minutes.

The proposed foundation for this automation is centralized information management: an **asset manager** containing system information and a **risk manager** containing collected risk data. AI could then analyze this consolidated information and automate decisions and workflows. The speaker identifies fragmented legacy systems and distributed ownership as major barriers.

The keynote concludes with a pragmatic view of research priorities. Some areas, such as adversarial machine learning and sensor attacks against advanced driver-assistance systems, are technically interesting and may scale through physical artifacts such as 3D-printed road patches, but the speaker notes that there is no evidence in the transcript that these attacks are being used in the wild. Intrusion-detection systems are also viewed skeptically because of false-alarm problems, although behavioral detection may become more valuable for autonomous vehicles, particularly when combined with observations of the physical environment.

# Key technical ideas

1. **Shift from technological capability to operational efficiency**
   - Automotive security is no longer limited primarily by the absence of security technologies.
   - The larger problem is the cost, manual effort, coordination, and lack of scalability of security processes.

2. **Security as the primary objective, compliance as the byproduct**
   - Regulation forces organizations to perform security-related activities.
   - Treating compliance as the main objective can produce expensive, checklist-oriented processes.
   - A more efficient security strategy could satisfy compliance requirements naturally.

3. **Attack economics determines practical risk**
   - Automotive systems may be technically vulnerable without being attractive targets.
   - Attackers generally pursue targets that provide financial returns and can be attacked at scale.
   - Vehicle theft and chip tuning remain important because they offer direct incentives.

4. **AI lowers the cost of vulnerability research**
   - AI can assist with firmware debugging, reverse engineering, and vulnerability discovery.
   - The attacker may still need physical access to extract firmware, but AI can automate much of the subsequent analysis.
   - Lower attack costs may make previously unprofitable targets attractive.

5. **AI-enabled defensive automation**
   - Potential applications include:
     - Vulnerability discovery and patching.
     - Vehicle security operations centers.
     - Pattern and anomaly detection.
     - Automated security-product development.
     - Risk analysis.
     - Firewall-rule updates.
     - Security operations and compliance support.

6. **Compressed remediation timelines**
   - Current vulnerability response can take months because of supplier coordination, purchasing, safety validation, testing, and deployment.
   - The proposed direction is to reduce remediation from months to hours.
   - Firewall changes should potentially occur within minutes.

7. **Centralized asset and risk knowledge**
   - An asset manager could consolidate information about vehicle components and systems.
   - A risk manager could consolidate risk assessments and related security information.
   - AI could use these unified sources to support automated decisions and workflows.
   - Legacy systems and distributed stakeholder ownership are identified as major obstacles.

8. **Scale is broader than wireless connectivity**
   - Wireless attacks are important because they can affect fleets.
   - However, physical attacks can also scale if the same artifact or technique affects many vehicles—for example, road-based adversarial artifacts targeting vehicle perception systems.

9. **Software-defined vehicles create both opportunities and risks**
   - Consolidating many electronic control units into a few powerful computing platforms may enable stronger, more mature security mechanisms.
   - The same consolidation may increase the importance of protecting those central platforms.

10. **Autonomous fleets may have stronger attacker incentives**
    - Autonomous vehicles require persistent connectivity and may depend on remote assistance.
    - Fleet operators are likely to have substantial financial and reputational exposure to downtime.
    - This may make autonomous fleets more attractive targets than conventional individually owned vehicles.

11. **Limitations of current intrusion detection**
    - The speaker reports that many vehicle IDS approaches suffer from excessive false alarms.
    - Behavioral IDS may become more useful for autonomous vehicles if it can assess whether remote instructions are consistent with the physical environment and expected vehicle behavior.

12. **AI does not close every security gap**
    - AI cannot by itself solve physical-access requirements, fragmented organizational processes, weak incentives, or legacy-system integration.
    - It may improve both attacks and defenses, creating pressure for the automotive industry to automate defensive processes rapidly.
