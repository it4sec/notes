## Summary

This session examines how artificial intelligence is moving from experimentation toward operational deployment in healthcare. The speakers focus less on speculative AI capabilities and more on practical implementation: clinical documentation, medical imaging, prior authorization, scheduling, administrative work, workforce transformation, and the redesign of healthcare processes.

A central theme is the distinction between **automating individual tasks** and **replacing entire professions**. The panel argues that current healthcare AI is highly useful for narrow, repetitive, or pattern-recognition tasks, but remains insufficient for fully autonomous clinical decision-making. Successful implementation therefore requires human oversight, validation, monitoring, and redesign of existing workflows.

### Current deployment at Cleveland Clinic

David describes Cleveland Clinic as having made a substantial organizational commitment to AI, including the appointment of a chief AI officer approximately one year before the session. The organization has moved beyond limited experimentation in several areas:

- **AI-assisted clinical documentation:** Approximately 5,000 Cleveland Clinic providers received AI scribing software on their iPhones and are using it to document patient encounters. David characterizes this deployment as highly successful.
- **Medical imaging interpretation:** Imaging is identified as a major current area of work.
- **Prior authorization and appeals:** AI is being developed to generate the documentation needed for prior authorization requests and appeals, particularly for specialists such as medical geneticists. The intended benefit is to reduce administrative effort and allow clinicians to spend more time on higher-value activities.
- **Scheduling:** David identifies patient scheduling as another area with substantial automation potential. AI could potentially match patients to the appropriate scheduling protocols more rapidly than centralized staff working through referral rules manually.
- **Business and administrative functions:** The panel notes that additional AI activity is occurring on the business side of healthcare, although the speakers do not describe all of those projects in detail.

### Radiology as a leading AI application area

Ben explains that radiology has been one of the medical specialties most affected by AI. He states that more than 70% of newly produced FDA-approved healthcare AI software is related to radiology.

The discussion describes an evolution from narrow diagnostic tools to systems capable of identifying multiple findings:

- Earlier systems were often designed for a single binary task, such as determining whether pneumonia was present.
- Newer systems can identify many conditions at once. As an example, Ben refers to software acquired by Sectra from Eastern Europe that can reportedly diagnose approximately 75 different findings on chest radiographs.

However, the speakers emphasize that broad capability does not automatically mean robust clinical performance. A system that works well on outpatient chest radiographs may perform poorly on severely ill intensive-care patients whose images contain difficult positioning, lines, enlarged hearts, or extensive lung abnormalities. The technical implication is that AI systems must be evaluated across the full range of clinical conditions in which they will be used, rather than only on relatively clean or representative cases.

### Pattern recognition versus clinical knowledge

The panel distinguishes between current AI systems as highly capable **pattern-recognition engines** and the broader knowledge required for clinical interpretation.

Current systems can rapidly recognize visual patterns, but the speakers argue that radiologists also rely on:

- Anatomy
- Pathology
- Physiology
- Clinical context
- Medical knowledge acquired through training and experience

Ben argues that present-day AI is not yet close to replacing radiologists because many systems lack an integrated model of these underlying concepts. He points to efforts to incorporate physical or world models into AI and suggests that analogous models in healthcare would need to include anatomy, pathology, and physiology.

This would represent a significant technical transition: from systems that primarily match patterns in data to systems that combine pattern recognition with structured medical knowledge and more reliable reasoning. The speakers do not predict when such systems will become available or whether adding this knowledge will preserve the speed and performance of current models.

### Automation, augmentation, and disruption

The session presents disruption as a gradual process rather than a single event. The speakers emphasize that AI may first replace tasks, not people.

Examples include:

- Automatic transcription of radiology reports
- Tracing contours in imaging studies
- Calculating measures such as left ventricular ejection fraction
- Generating prior authorization documents
- Coding and billing
- Scheduling
- Administrative and repetitive healthcare operations

Ben gives the example of radiology transcriptionists whose work was eliminated after voice-recognition software became integrated into a new PACS workflow. Radiologists reportedly benefited from immediate transcription rather than waiting for reports to return later for approval, but the transcriptionist role itself disappeared.

The panel also discusses the idea that healthcare professionals who learn to use AI may displace those who do not. This frames near-term competition not only as AI versus humans, but also as **AI-enabled workers versus workers who do not effectively use AI**.

### Workforce and deskilling concerns

The speakers identify several risks associated with AI adoption:

- **Deskilling:** Medical students and trainees may lose opportunities to develop diagnostic and interviewing skills if AI performs too much of the initial work.
- **Misinformation in electronic health records:** AI systems that summarize EHR data or generate diagnoses may reproduce inaccurate information already present in the record.
- **Loss of clinical learning:** Traditional interactions among interns, residents, consultants, and patients help trainees learn how to ask questions and distinguish reliable from unreliable information. The panel questions whether AI-mediated workflows will preserve this learning.
- **Devaluation of work:** Employees may question their value when machines perform tasks that previously defined their jobs.
- **Black-box decision-making:** AI systems may produce answers without providing sufficiently clear explanations of how those answers were generated.
- **Workflow dependence on changing models:** Commercial AI platforms can change frequently, meaning that organizations building processes on top of them may be working with a constantly changing technical foundation.

Ted stresses that human beings should not be treated as interchangeable components. In his view, organizations should value the people who design, monitor, and improve automated workflows, even when the individual tasks within those workflows are automated.

### AI reliability, learning, and reasoning

The panel repeatedly states that current systems are not yet reliable enough to replace humans in healthcare. The speakers discuss systems that can still be wrong frequently, while also emphasizing that model performance is improving rapidly.

Several technical requirements are identified:

- Systems should provide explanatory information about why they reached a conclusion.
- Errors should be detected, analyzed, and used to improve future behavior.
- AI should incorporate reasoning rather than relying solely on statistical pattern matching.
- Organizations need mechanisms to test whether model updates improve or degrade performance.
- Clinical AI should remain argumentative or assistive rather than operating as an unexplained black-box diagnostic authority.

Ted describes commercial AI platforms as a rapidly changing landscape, with major model updates and new features appearing frequently. This creates an operational challenge: healthcare workflows built around a model may change when the underlying model changes.

The speakers’ preferred direction is collaborative human–AI operation. They argue that healthcare AI should be introduced by examining and rebuilding workflows, with humans remaining involved in design, validation, supervision, and patient-centered decision-making.

### Cost reduction and incentives

The discussion questions whether AI is currently reducing healthcare costs and, if so, for whom. The speakers note that:

- AI may reduce labor or administrative costs.
- AI-enabled billing and coding can increase healthcare spending rather than decrease it.
- Prior authorization may become an AI-mediated contest between insurers and providers rather than a net reduction in system-wide costs.
- Savings depend on the goals and incentives built into the implementation.
- A large proportion of AI proofs of concept and experiments reportedly fail, making disciplined planning important.

Ted argues that organizations should begin with a clear objective and design the implementation backward from the desired outcome. They should determine which existing processes can be retained, which must be discarded, and how the new workflow affects patients and families.

The session also emphasizes that a workflow that is efficient for an organization may still be difficult for patients, particularly older adults or people dealing with illness. Patient and family involvement is therefore presented as an essential part of responsible AI implementation.

## Technical innovation and authors’ ideas

The session’s technical contribution is primarily conceptual and implementation-oriented rather than a report of a new algorithm or experimental study. Its core ideas concern how healthcare organizations should evaluate, deploy, and govern increasingly capable AI systems.

The speakers propose a progression:

1. **Narrow task automation**
   - AI performs a tightly defined function such as detecting pneumonia, transcribing a report, tracing an anatomical contour, or calculating a measurement.

2. **Multi-finding systems**
   - AI expands from one diagnostic target to many possible findings, such as systems that identify dozens of abnormalities on a chest radiograph.

3. **Context-sensitive clinical systems**
   - Systems must perform reliably across different patient populations, imaging conditions, and levels of clinical complexity.

4. **Knowledge-integrated models**
   - AI should incorporate medical concepts such as anatomy, pathology, physiology, and clinical context rather than relying only on statistical visual similarity.

5. **Reasoning and explainability**
   - Systems should provide interpretable rationales, identify uncertainty, and support safe human review.

6. **Human–AI workflow collaboration**
   - AI should be embedded into redesigned clinical and administrative processes, with humans responsible for oversight, correction, and patient-centered judgment.

# Key technical ideas

- **Deploy AI where the task is narrow, repetitive, and measurable.** Documentation, transcription, scheduling, authorization support, and image measurements are presented as practical entry points.
- **Distinguish task replacement from professional replacement.** Automating a task does not necessarily eliminate the broader role of a clinician.
- **Validate models under real clinical conditions.** Performance on clean outpatient images may not transfer to ICU cases or other complex settings.
- **Move beyond pattern matching.** Future medical AI needs integrated knowledge of anatomy, pathology, physiology, and clinical context.
- **Develop reasoning-oriented systems.** The panel views reasoning as distinct from merely producing plausible outputs based on learned patterns.
- **Require explainability and error analysis.** AI should indicate why it reached a conclusion, expose failure modes, and support learning from mistakes.
- **Continuously monitor commercial models.** Frequent updates to foundation models can alter the behavior of healthcare applications built on top of them.
- **Keep clinical AI assistive and accountable.** The speakers favor augmentation and human control over unexplained autonomous diagnosis.
- **Redesign workflows rather than simply adding software.** Effective implementation requires examining every step of a process and determining what should be retained, automated, or rebuilt.
- **Treat humans as part of the technical system.** Healthcare workers are needed to design, supervise, validate, and improve AI-enabled workflows.
- **Evaluate incentives and patient impact.** Cost savings for an organization do not necessarily translate into lower costs for patients or the healthcare system.
- **Include patients and families in implementation decisions.** A technically efficient process may still be difficult or inappropriate for people navigating illness or limited technical ability.
