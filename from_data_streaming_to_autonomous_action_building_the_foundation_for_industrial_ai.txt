## Summary

The discussion examines why industrial AI often succeeds in isolated pilots but struggles to become a scalable, autonomous operational capability. The central argument is that manufacturers have made substantial progress in collecting industrial data, but their architectures remain oriented toward historical analytics rather than real-time operational intelligence.

The panel identifies the missing foundation as a combination of:

1. A reusable industrial data and integration layer  
2. Rich operational context and semantic meaning  
3. Edge-capable execution for time-critical decisions  
4. Governance, identity, access control, auditability, and rollback  
5. A gradual, risk-based path from human assistance to autonomy  

The speakers distinguish between using AI to analyze historical data and using AI agents to respond to live industrial conditions. Historian-centric architectures are considered adequate for storing data and supporting retrospective analysis, but insufficient for real-time, multi-application, agentic operations. The proposed alternative is an infrastructure-centric architecture in which applications consume shared, contextualized data through a common foundation rather than repeatedly connecting directly to individual machines and systems.

A major technical issue is contextualization. Jonathan Alexander describes manufacturing context as the knowledge of how equipment, processes, recipes, product grades, and people’s operating practices relate to one another. Much of this knowledge remains in the heads of subject-matter experts. Kudzai Manditereza emphasizes that contextualization is not only about cleaning or structuring data; it is about giving data meaning. For example, a “work order” may have different meanings and downstream implications in different companies or processes.

Peter Sorowka challenges the assumption that industrial AI must wait for perfectly clean data. He argues that modern large language models can handle messy, unstructured inputs more effectively than earlier machine-learning systems. However, the other panelists clarify that models still require sufficient domain context to understand proprietary manufacturing processes, engineering drawings, equipment relationships, and operational consequences. The discussion therefore converges on a distinction between data cleanliness and semantic meaning: perfect data preparation is not always necessary, but relevant context is.

The panel also presents hybrid edge-cloud architectures as the practical direction for industrial AI. Edge execution is favored when latency, data volume, or operational reliability makes cloud round trips unsuitable. Cloud execution remains appropriate for model training, historical analysis, benchmarking, and non-real-time reporting. The speakers note that the proper boundary depends on the industrial site, the number of plants involved, network conditions, and the criticality of the process.

Scalability is presented as an infrastructure and repeatability problem rather than simply an AI-model problem. A successful pilot may depend on plant-specific engineering effort, but replication can fail because other plants use different PLC vendors, network configurations, security policies, and equipment. David Purón argues that industrial AI needs an orchestration layer capable of zero-touch provisioning, portable applications and models, and batch management across multiple facilities. This would transform rollout from a bespoke engineering project into a more repeatable software distribution and configuration process.

On autonomy, the panel rejects an abrupt transition to fully autonomous control. Instead, the speakers describe a staged model:

- AI informs a human decision-maker.
- AI proposes an action that requires human approval.
- AI acts while a human supervises in real time.
- AI operates with human review of exceptions or periodic samples.
- Fully autonomous operation may be considered only for suitable, sufficiently understood problems.

The panel strongly differentiates agentic AI from conventional AI. Conventional machine-learning models remain valuable for prediction, detection, and recommendation. Agentic AI becomes useful when a problem requires a chain of actions, such as detecting an anomaly, retrieving relevant data, consulting equipment documentation, checking maintenance history, and coordinating the next operational step. The speakers recommend beginning with the operational problem rather than selecting agentic AI simply because it is technologically fashionable.

The human and organizational dimensions are treated as equally important to the technology. Operational subject-matter experts—such as automation engineers, maintenance teams, SCADA engineers, and process engineers—should remain responsible for supervising AI systems. However, industrial personnel may not be IT, Linux, Kubernetes, or networking specialists. The panel therefore emphasizes making AI infrastructure accessible to OT personnel as part of IT/OT convergence.

The expected outcomes include improvements to existing manufacturing KPIs, such as asset utilization, quality, reliability, throughput, and other operational measures. The speakers cite examples discussed during the session, including reported savings from classic AI in water treatment and reported improvements in selected industrial KPIs. The panel also describes a root-cause-analysis use case in which a system combines maintenance records, process data, documentation, procedures, and contextual information to produce an investigation result much faster than traditional manual analysis.

The final conclusion is that the main barrier is not necessarily the availability of AI technology. More difficult challenges include organizational ownership, OT/IT alignment, plant-versus-corporate governance, standardization versus local autonomy, safety, accountability, and trust. The authors’ overall position is that industrial AI should be built on durable infrastructure and governance while allowing models and intelligence layers to evolve over time.

## Key technical ideas

### 1. Replace historian-centric architectures with operational data foundations

Traditional architectures commonly send machine data into a historian or data lake for later analysis. The panel argues that this limits AI to retrospective use.

A more capable architecture would expose industrial data through a shared infrastructure layer that supports:

- Real-time distribution
- Multiple data consumers
- Operational applications
- AI models and agents
- Potentially controlled interaction with production systems

This changes the data layer from passive storage into an operational interface.

### 2. Move from application-centric to infrastructure-centric integration

In an application-centric design, each AI or software use case connects directly to its required machines and systems. This creates repeated connectivity work and locks contextualization inside individual applications.

An infrastructure-centric design provides a reusable abstraction layer:

- Applications connect to the shared infrastructure.
- The infrastructure connects to industrial data sources.
- Context and integration work can be reused across use cases.
- The same foundation can be deployed across plants with different equipment.

This is presented as a central requirement for scaling beyond a single pilot.

### 3. Treat context as semantic meaning, not only data cleanliness

The discussion distinguishes three related concepts:

- **Structure:** Whether data is organized and technically readable
- **Cleanliness:** Whether data has been labeled, normalized, or corrected
- **Meaning:** What the data represents within a particular industrial process

The panel argues that large language models can often work with imperfect or unstructured inputs, but agents still need domain meaning to reason about consequences, relationships, and operational dependencies. Knowledge graphs and semantic models are proposed as ways to represent this meaning.

### 4. Preserve model independence through modular architectures

Industrial data foundations should outlast individual AI models, vendors, and tools. The recommended approach is to separate relatively stable elements from rapidly changing ones:

- Manufacturing processes and business purposes are comparatively stable.
- Data organization and connectivity form a durable foundation.
- Models, algorithms, and visualization layers will change more quickly.

Modular architecture allows a model or intelligence component to be replaced without rebuilding the entire industrial data system.

### 5. Use hybrid edge-cloud deployment

The panel identifies three main factors for deciding whether an AI workload belongs at the edge:

1. **Latency:** Whether the system must react in real time  
2. **Data volume:** Whether transmitting all data to the cloud is impractical or expensive  
3. **Reliability:** Whether operation must continue independently of corporate or wide-area networks  

Edge execution is associated with autonomous operational control and time-sensitive industrial processes. Cloud execution is considered suitable for historical analysis, training, benchmarking, and non-real-time reporting.

### 6. Build an orchestration layer for multi-plant scalability

Scaling from one factory to many requires infrastructure that can absorb plant heterogeneity. The proposed capabilities include:

- Zero-touch provisioning
- Deployment of protocol connectors
- Portable applications and AI models
- Containerized or otherwise portable infrastructure
- Batch configuration and change management
- Centralized governance with local operational flexibility

The objective is to make multi-site rollout resemble software distribution and configuration rather than repeating a custom engineering project at every plant.

### 7. Apply agentic AI to operational exceptions

The panel positions agentic AI as especially suitable for exception handling rather than replacing deterministic control systems. An agent could:

- Receive an anomaly signal from a predictive model
- Investigate related operational data
- Retrieve documentation
- Check maintenance history
- Create or update a work order
- Recommend next steps
- Request human approval at defined points

This creates a division of labor in which deterministic systems manage control, predictive models generate signals, and agents coordinate multi-step investigation and response.

### 8. Introduce graduated autonomy

The proposed autonomy model is based on increasing levels of delegation and supervision:

- Human-led, AI-informed
- Human approval required before action
- Human supervision while AI acts
- Human review by exception or sampling
- Potential full autonomy for narrowly defined, sufficiently trusted tasks

The appropriate level depends on risk tolerance, operational criticality, and the consequences of an incorrect action.

### 9. Govern AI as an industrial actor

If AI can influence production, it requires more than model accuracy. The panel identifies the need for:

- AI identity
- Access-control rules
- Policies limiting actions
- Audit logging
- Traceability of decisions and actions
- Versioning
- Rollback capability
- Clear human responsibility

These mechanisms are described as necessary for maintaining control as AI systems become more autonomous.

### 10. Keep AI out of unsuitable deterministic control loops

The participants express caution about placing large language models directly inside critical control systems. Their concerns include non-deterministic behavior and the difficulty of guaranteeing predictable process execution.

The suggested pattern is to use AI externally to support investigation, recommendations, orchestration, and operational decisions while retaining deterministic control for core machine and process loops.

### 11. Start with the operational problem, not the newest technology

A recurring recommendation is to select the simplest technology that solves the problem:

- Classic AI remains effective for prediction and optimization.
- Agentic AI is justified when the task requires multi-step reasoning and coordination.
- The OT organization should help define the use case and lead operational adoption.
- AI initiatives should be measured against existing manufacturing KPIs.

### 12. Make AI operable by OT personnel

Industrial AI cannot depend entirely on specialized IT teams after deployment. The people operating the plant must be able to supervise and troubleshoot the system.

The panel therefore frames IT/OT convergence as making IT capabilities usable by OT teams, including personnel responsible for automation, maintenance, SCADA, and process engineering.
