## Overview

The presentation argues that healthcare cybersecurity must evolve from a narrow focus on preventing intrusion toward **cyber resilience**, defined primarily as preserving continuity of care, patient safety, institutional trust, and the ability to recover rapidly after disruption.

Finney’s central premise is that the traditional healthcare security model—protecting a known, internally managed data-center perimeter—is no longer adequate. Healthcare workflows now span cloud platforms, SaaS ecosystems, AI-enabled decision support, connected medical devices, APIs, remote staff, and extensive machine-to-machine communication. As a result, she argues that healthcare security operations must become more identity-centered, behavior-aware, context-sensitive, and closely integrated with clinical and business continuity planning.

The presentation emphasizes that cybersecurity incidents in healthcare should be treated as potential **patient-care disruptions**, not merely IT events. This changes how organizations should prioritize architecture, investment, incident response, third-party risk, and recovery planning.

## Main Technical and Strategic Contributions

### 1. Reframing cyber resilience around continuity of care

Finney’s most important conceptual shift is from attack prevention to operational resilience. A resilient healthcare security program must be able to:

- Preserve clinical operations during cyber incidents.
- Recover systems quickly.
- Contain risks originating from third parties.
- Protect patient safety.
- Maintain institutional and patient trust.
- Coordinate cybersecurity with emergency response and business continuity functions.

She recommends exercises and tabletop scenarios involving security, emergency response, and business continuity teams so organizations can understand the effect of cyber incidents on patient care.

### 2. Replacing the traditional perimeter with identity

The presentation describes the healthcare perimeter as effectively dissolved because systems, staff, devices, applications, and services operate across distributed cloud and remote environments.

Finney identifies **identity as the new perimeter**. This includes not only human users but also:

- Service accounts.
- API keys.
- OAuth tokens.
- Cloud workflows.
- AI agents.
- Automation platforms.
- Medical-device integrations.
- Trusted workload relationships.

This requires security operations to move from an infrastructure-centric model to an **identity-and-behavioral model**.

### 3. Addressing non-human identity and credential sprawl

A major technical theme is the growth of non-human identities. Finney argues that these identities may already outnumber human identities in healthcare ecosystems.

This creates risks associated with:

- Excessive or poorly understood service accounts.
- Credential sprawl.
- Compromised API keys.
- Misused OAuth tokens.
- Automated workloads operating without obvious human oversight.
- Trusted relationships between systems.

Because these identities perform critical functions and may access protected health information, security teams need greater visibility into their behavior and relationships.

### 4. Treating APIs as critical healthcare infrastructure

Finney characterizes APIs as a major contemporary attack surface because they support:

- Electronic health record integrations.
- AI interfaces.
- Telehealth.
- Cloud application connectivity.
- Interoperability.
- Medical-device communication.

She argues that API security must become a core operational capability. This includes the ability to:

- Discover APIs and associated credentials.
- Monitor API activity at runtime.
- Apply behavioral analytics to API keys and connections.
- Detect violations of expected trust relationships.
- Investigate potentially compromised tokens and credentials.

The presentation connects this requirement to the broader shift from perimeter monitoring to monitoring relationships and behavior across distributed systems.

### 5. Using AI for defense while recognizing its risks

Finney presents AI as both an accelerator of cyber risk and a potential tool for defense.

She notes that threat actors are adopting AI, including for the development of exploits. At the same time, healthcare security teams need AI strategies and tools to process the growing volume and complexity of security events.

Potential defensive uses discussed include helping security operations:

- Analyze identity and behavioral data.
- Identify anomalous API or token activity.
- Evaluate access context.
- Assist with decisions about isolating potentially compromised devices.
- Support monitoring of AI interactions, data flows, and API usage.

However, she does not advocate unrestricted automation. In clinical environments, a security action such as isolating a device could interfere with an active procedure. She therefore expects a **human in the loop** to remain necessary, particularly where automated action could affect patient care.

### 6. Establishing AI governance as security governance

Finney argues that AI adoption is moving faster than organizational governance. She also compares this situation with the longstanding healthcare practice of deploying large numbers of applications that may not be fully governed.

Her key governance position is that security operations must have a seat at the AI governance table. Security teams need visibility into:

- AI interactions.
- Data flows.
- API usage.
- AI-assisted behavior.
- Potentially anomalous or malicious AI activity.
- The difference between approved and potentially harmful AI behavior.

The concern is not limited to explicitly unauthorized tools. Finney emphasizes that malicious activity may operate “low and slow,” remaining inside an environment for as long as possible while avoiding obvious detection.

### 7. Moving beyond static role-based access control

The presentation argues that traditional role-based access control is insufficient for modern healthcare environments because it assumes:

- Static roles.
- Stable environments.
- Predictable workflows.

Finney proposes a move toward **attribute-based access control (ABAC)** and dynamic, contextual security decisions. Relevant attributes include:

- Device posture.
- User behavior.
- Workload identity.
- Clinical context.
- Location.
- Data being accessed.
- Whether protected health information is involved.
- API gateway familiarity.
- Whether the workflow is approved.
- Whether the access pattern is anomalous.

The goal is to support a zero-trust architecture in which access decisions are based on current risk and context rather than solely on a preassigned role.

### 8. Designing for third-party compromise

Finney uses healthcare disruptions associated in the transcript with Change Healthcare and “Strack” as examples of ecosystem and identity risk.

The Change Healthcare discussion illustrates how dependence on external platforms can affect:

- Pharmacy operations.
- Reimbursement workflows.
- Scheduling.
- Broader care delivery.

The “Strack” example is presented as an identity-focused compromise affecting cloud-connected environments, workstations, ordering systems, and supply chains.

From these examples, Finney concludes that healthcare resilience must assume that third-party compromises are possible, perhaps inevitable. Organizations need procedures for:

- Assessing whether a compromised vendor is contained.
- Determining when connectivity should be restored.
- Establishing confidence that reconnection will not extend the impact.
- Managing third-party containment and operational recovery.

### 9. Applying layered defense to unstable environments

Finney supports a multi-layered defense strategy but notes that implementation is difficult when organizations are modernizing legacy environments while systems and workflows continue to change.

The proposed layers include:

- Identity protection.
- API discovery and runtime monitoring.
- Behavioral analytics.
- Dynamic, contextual access decisions.
- AI governance.
- Human oversight of high-impact security actions.
- Third-party risk and recovery planning.
- Coordination with clinical continuity and emergency response programs.

## Key Technical Ideas

1. **Cyber resilience means preserving continuity of care, not merely blocking attacks.**
2. **Identity has replaced the traditional network perimeter** in distributed healthcare environments.
3. **Security operations should shift from infrastructure-centric to identity-and-behavioral analysis.**
4. **Non-human identities are a primary security domain**, including service accounts, API keys, OAuth tokens, AI agents, and automated workloads.
5. **APIs should be treated as critical healthcare infrastructure** and monitored for runtime behavior, trust-relationship violations, and credential compromise.
6. **AI is needed to help security teams keep pace with threats**, but it also introduces governance, monitoring, and abuse challenges.
7. **AI governance is part of security governance** because AI affects data flows, access, APIs, and operational behavior.
8. **Static role-based access control is inadequate** for dynamic cloud, AI, and clinical environments.
9. **Attribute-based and contextual access control** should evaluate device posture, user behavior, clinical context, location, data sensitivity, workload identity, and workflow legitimacy.
10. **Human oversight remains essential** when automated security actions could interrupt clinical procedures or patient care.
11. **Third-party compromise should be treated as an expected scenario**, requiring containment, recovery, and reconnection criteria.
12. **Security performance should ultimately be measured by trust, continuity of care, and safe innovation**, not only by detection, response, and containment.

## Overall Assessment

The presentation’s technical innovation lies less in proposing a single product or algorithm than in defining a new operating model for healthcare cybersecurity. Finney connects identity security, API protection, AI governance, behavioral analytics, clinical context, and business continuity into one resilience-oriented framework.

Her central idea is that healthcare security decisions must account for operational and clinical consequences. In this model, the most effective security operation is not necessarily the one that blocks the most activity, but the one that can distinguish harmful behavior from legitimate clinical workflows, respond proportionately, preserve patient safety, and help the organization continue caring for patients during disruption.
