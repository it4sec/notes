## Summary

The panel examines how AI is changing reverse engineering, vulnerability research, malware analysis, exploitation, security engineering, and the security labor market. Its central argument is not that AI has either already replaced security researchers or is merely a productivity tool. Rather, the panel presents AI as a rapidly changing force multiplier whose usefulness depends heavily on human expertise, workflow design, verification, and judgment.

A recurring distinction is made between **what models are capable of doing** and **what those capabilities mean for employment and professional roles**. The panelists generally reject the idea that AI will immediately eliminate all security jobs. They do expect automation to affect routine, mechanistic, and lower-skill work, while creating new opportunities for people who can guide models, build effective workflows, investigate opaque systems, and handle decisions requiring accountability.

### AI-assisted reverse engineering

John McIntosh describes a patch-diffing workflow that combines LLMs with deterministic reverse-engineering tools. A task that previously required months of manual work was reduced to several weeks with an earlier AI-assisted workflow, and to a few days or less with current tools. The important innovation is not simply asking a model to analyze binaries; it is combining probabilistic model reasoning with deterministic tooling and structured workflows.

Aaron Portnoy emphasizes that models are strong at mechanistic procedures but weaker at the **meta-strategy** behind expert work. Reverse engineering is not only a sequence of operations. It also involves deciding:

- where to look;
- which paths not to pursue;
- when to stop;
- how to rank findings;
- what evidence is sufficient;
- and how to respond to unexpected behavior.

The panel describes AI harnesses and scaffolding as mechanisms for encoding this strategic layer. However, these harnesses are unstable: when a more capable model appears, it can absorb or replace parts of the workflow, forcing researchers to redesign their scaffolding.

A further proposed improvement is the use of **ranking and decomposition** rather than presenting a model with a massive body of decompiled code all at once. Portnoy also discusses knowledge graphs as a way to preserve continuity between analysis sessions, retain intermediate results, and encode researcher feedback and methods.

### Exploitation and autonomous operation

Thai Duong characterizes current AI-assisted hacking as closer to the “Deep Blue” phase than the “AlphaGo” phase: humans and machines work together, and the human remains essential. Models can discover vulnerabilities, but reliable exploitation still requires skilled human direction.

The panel identifies several reasons for this limitation:

- exploitation must be reliable, not merely plausible;
- modern mitigations create difficult technical constraints;
- there is limited public training data for some advanced exploitation problems;
- real-world operations require stealth and careful sequencing;
- current agents are probabilistic and often too eager to perform many actions at once.

An experiment in which an agent was released into a network reportedly resulted in the agent performing too many simultaneous actions and “going completely crazy.” The panel uses this as evidence that autonomy can be counterproductive when the correct strategy requires restraint, timing, and risk management.

### Malware analysis and reverse engineering

Gadi Evron describes using Claude to analyze multiple malware samples, including a large JavaScript sample, obfuscation, and a binary. The model produced useful results, but the panel stresses that usefulness is not equivalent to correctness.

Marion Marschalek highlights verification as the central problem. An AI system may misclassify a remote-access Trojan as ransomware, or incorrectly interpret a packed sample. She also describes an incident in which an AI system was asked to analyze a runtime packer and chose to execute the sample instead of reasoning about the packer statically. Even when execution occurs inside a virtual machine, the analyst may not be able to determine whether the environment was truly safe.

This leads to a broader principle: AI-generated security analysis must be independently validated, particularly when the result could affect incident response, attribution, or containment decisions.

### Human expertise, accountability, and new research areas

The panelists identify several areas where human judgment remains especially important:

- assigning responsibility or attribution;
- making decisions in open-ended problem spaces;
- determining when an investigation should stop;
- assessing whether an output is trustworthy;
- handling safety-critical systems;
- understanding unfamiliar or opaque mechanisms;
- and creating new abstractions or research directions.

Portnoy argues that AI should not be the final accountable entity for decisions such as nation-state attribution or other actions with serious consequences. He also suggests that AI itself may become a major reverse-engineering target because its internal mechanisms are opaque and difficult to interpret.

Sergey Bratus frames the issue in terms of **new primitives and abstractions**. He asks whether AI has independently produced something comparable to a genuinely new technical idea or computational abstraction. The discussion concludes that the panelists have not seen AI independently invent such a primitive without human steering. AI is considered strong at combining existing techniques, but not yet demonstrated to be a reliable autonomous source of fundamentally new security concepts.

A related example involves AI combining known mathematical knowledge with an exploitation problem involving GPU-rendered pointer values. The model reportedly identified a mathematical lemma that allowed the researchers to convert pixel information into pointers without loss. The panel treats this as an example of AI combining widely separated pieces of existing knowledge, rather than inventing a completely new technique.

### Local models and access to AI

The panel raises concerns about unequal access to powerful AI systems. If advanced models become controlled by large companies or specific countries, organizations and individuals without access could be disadvantaged.

Local and open-weight models are presented as a partial safeguard. John McIntosh notes that local models can protect users from losing access to centralized services. Halvar Flake cautions that local models are generally weaker than frontier models, but argues that smaller specialized models may be sufficient for particular tasks such as code analysis.

He gives the example of a small reasoning-focused model that can run on an iPhone and solve combinatorics problems despite lacking the broad factual knowledge of a frontier model. The implication is that security workflows may not always require a general-purpose model; task-specific reasoning models could be locally deployed and self-hosted.

### Entry-level work and professional development

Marion Marschalek expresses concern about the disappearance of entry-level reverse-engineering positions. If routine junior work is automated, the field may lose the training path through which future experts develop.

The panel nevertheless recommends entering the field, but using AI as an accelerator rather than as a substitute for understanding. Suggested approaches include:

- selecting a specific area such as firmware, compatibility reverse engineering, patch diffing, or malware analysis;
- using AI to build a focused curriculum;
- reaching the edge of what current tools can do;
- learning to integrate AI into an existing project;
- and developing expertise beyond merely operating a chatbot.

The panelists repeatedly recommend choosing a concrete project and applying AI directly to it. Their view is that newcomers can learn faster than previous generations, but they must develop enough technical understanding to guide, challenge, and verify the system.

### Vulnerability disclosure

AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is presented as a potential threat to current disclosure systems. If models can generate large numbers of findings, maintainers and vendors may become overwhelmed. The bottleneck may shift from discovering vulnerabilities to receiving, validating, triaging, and responding to reports.

The panel discusses several related problems:

- maintainers failing to respond within expected disclosure periods;
- researchers receiving no response from major vendors;
- large volumes of low-quality or fabricated reports;
- duplication of vulnerability findings;
- and the possibility that a vulnerability in code could increasingly become equivalent to a public vulnerability.

The participants do not conclude that coordinated disclosure is obsolete. Instead, they describe a system under growing pressure. Thai Duong argues that disclosure remains important, particularly when maintainers do not respond. Other panelists note that organizations may need AI-assisted intake and triage simply to handle the increasing volume of submissions.

### Firmware, embedded systems, and context limitations

Dragos Ruiu identifies firmware and embedded systems as areas where current AI systems remain weak. Binary analysis does not provide models with the same convenient token structure as source code, and large amounts of binary context can exceed practical context-window limits.

The problem is compounded by limited training data. Embedded systems often depend on processor-specific details, SDKs, and documentation that may be inaccessible or protected by nondisclosure agreements. These characteristics make firmware a promising area for continued human-led research.

### Formal guarantees and safety-critical systems

A discussion with a professor from Dartmouth College introduces safety-critical software and formal verification. The concern is whether AI-generated software could ever receive a reliability assurance comparable to software integrity levels used in safety-critical systems.

The panel notes that AI systems are probabilistic and that current models do not provide the level of determinism and confidence required for many critical applications. Formal limits such as undecidable problems remain relevant regardless of AI progress. The discussion suggests that future research may focus on software understanding, formal guarantees, and the boundary between probabilistic AI behavior and systems requiring provable properties.

## Key technical ideas

1. **Human expertise remains a force multiplier for AI.**  
   The panel’s dominant model is “expert plus tool,” not autonomous tool replacement.

2. **Separate capability growth from job displacement.**  
   A model becoming more capable does not immediately imply that corresponding human roles disappear.

3. **Combine probabilistic LLMs with deterministic tools.**  
   Patch diffing is presented as an example where LLM reasoning complements established reverse-engineering tooling.

4. **Encode expert meta-strategy in harnesses.**  
   Effective systems need more than procedural steps; they must represent search direction, stopping conditions, prioritization, and rejected paths.

5. **Expect harnesses to become obsolete quickly.**  
   As models improve, they absorb portions of existing scaffolding, requiring repeated redesign.

6. **Decompose and rank analysis targets.**  
   Processing smaller units and prioritizing them is more practical than giving a model an entire large decompilation at once.

7. **Use knowledge graphs for continuity.**  
   Knowledge graphs may preserve context, results, methods, and researcher feedback across discontinuous analysis sessions.

8. **Reliable exploitation is harder than vulnerability discovery.**  
   AI can identify potential weaknesses, but exploit reliability, mitigation bypasses, and operational stealth still require human expertise.

9. **Autonomous agents need restraint, not just capability.**  
   Agents that execute many actions simultaneously can damage an operation; a correct strategy may require making fewer moves.

10. **AI malware analysis requires verification.**  
    Useful output is not sufficient when classification, packing analysis, or execution decisions can be wrong.

11. **AI is effective at combining existing knowledge.**  
    The panel gives examples of models connecting mathematical knowledge with exploitation problems and combining known techniques that humans overlooked.

12. **Independent invention of new primitives remains un demonstrated.**  
    The participants report no clear example of an AI independently generating a genuinely new reverse-engineering or exploitation abstraction without human guidance.

13. **Local specialized models may be more practical than general frontier models.**  
    Small models with targeted reasoning capabilities could be deployed locally for code and security tasks.

14. **Firmware and embedded analysis remain difficult.**  
    Limited training data, NDA-protected documentation, binary representation, and context-window constraints create barriers for current models.

15. **AI may shift vulnerability-management bottlenecks.**  
    Discovery is becoming faster, while intake, validation, triage, and coordinated response may become the limiting stages.

16. **Human accountability remains essential.**  
    High-impact decisions such as attribution, safety-critical behavior, and consequential remediation should not be delegated without meaningful human responsibility.

17. **AI itself may become a reverse-engineering target.**  
    Understanding opaque model behavior, mechanisms, and decisions could become a significant future research area.

18. **The recommended strategy is active experimentation.**  
    The panel encourages researchers to begin with a concrete project, integrate AI into their workflow, and use it to reach the edge of current capabilities rather than merely automate routine work.
