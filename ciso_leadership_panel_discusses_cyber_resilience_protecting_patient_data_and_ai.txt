## Summary

The panel’s central idea is that healthcare cybersecurity must shift from periodic, perimeter-oriented protection toward continuous, organization-wide resilience. Healthcare systems are portrayed as unusually difficult to secure because they combine clinical technology, administrative applications, medical and IoT devices, cloud systems, vendor connections and numerous specialized business functions.

Anahi Santiago argues that security leadership must have a permanent role in organizational decision-making. A CISO who participates in strategic planning can anticipate developments such as hospital-at-home programs, virtual care, remote surgery using 5G, quantum computing and AI. Christiana Care reportedly plans five years ahead in order to prepare for its future operating environment. The panel nevertheless emphasizes that the threat environment changes so quickly that long-term planning must be supplemented by continuous adaptation.

The participants describe medical-device security as a major operational challenge. One panelist reports having approximately 90,000 IP addresses, of which about 70,000 are medical devices or IoT systems. These assets are difficult to manage because IT teams may not own them; clinical engineering often manages the devices, with information-security support. The panel describes a collaborative model in which a cybersecurity specialist is embedded within clinical engineering and clinical-engineering staff receive cybersecurity training.

Asset visibility is presented as a prerequisite for effective defense. Sharon describes an intake process requiring cybersecurity review for new network-connected devices, repeated scanning, and segmentation of medical-device environments by functions such as radiology, pharmacy and laboratory systems. This approach is intended to identify unauthorized or previously unknown devices. However, the panel notes that devices change rapidly and that complete IoT segmentation is difficult.

Legacy technology creates a further security and clinical-continuity dilemma. The discussion gives examples of Windows 7 and even Windows 2000 systems that remain in clinical use because a medical application or device was never updated for newer operating systems. One Windows 7 laptop was connected to a blood-monitoring system supporting a high-value, continuously running test. Shutting it down would have affected clinical operations and revenue, illustrating why vulnerable systems can remain active despite known security risks.

The panel distinguishes ordinary network segmentation from microsegmentation. Microsegmentation requires detailed knowledge of how systems communicate and what information is exchanged. Each medical-device type may require a profile defining legitimate traffic and distinguishing it from suspicious traffic. Because healthcare environments contain complex dependencies, constructing and maintaining these profiles is technically and operationally demanding.

A major portion of the discussion concerns the use of AI by attackers. Benoit Desjardins describes “Mythos,” identified in the transcript as a model associated with Anthropic’s Claude, as a general-purpose model with advanced cybersecurity capabilities. According to the discussion, it can identify vulnerabilities, generate exploits and support attack campaigns. The panel also refers to a Google Threat Intelligence Group report describing an AI-assisted zero-day exploit campaign involving a two-factor-authentication bypass flaw in unnamed open-source administrative software.

The panel’s broader argument is that AI lowers the expertise barrier and dramatically accelerates offensive activity. Desjardins cites an incident in which a Russian actor reportedly compromised 2,516 FortiGate devices across 106 countries using AI-based tools for attack planning and exploit generation. He also presents an illustrative comparison in which an AI-assisted attack can progress from exploit generation to full system compromise in approximately 73 seconds, whereas defensive processes may require minutes for detection and triage, an hour for prioritization and up to a day for patching.

This speed imbalance challenges traditional vulnerability-management practices. Sharon argues that healthcare organizations may need to reassess their policy of delaying patches on critical systems until extensive testing is complete. Two individually low-risk vulnerabilities may be combined into a critical exploit, meaning that conventional risk rankings may underestimate compound risk. In some situations, accepting planned downtime for patching may be safer than leaving a vulnerable clinical system operational.

The panel proposes resilience as the mechanism for making faster patching possible. Rather than relying only on fixed patch windows, healthcare organizations may need to move toward continuous or automated patching supported by redundancy, downtime procedures and repeated exercises. Possible approaches include maintaining additional devices or treatment capacity so that one MRI or other medical system can be removed for patching while patient care continues. The panel recognizes that such redundancy is expensive and may not always be practical; clearly defined downtime procedures and regular practice are presented as an alternative or complement.

The panel also challenges the assumption that adding more technology necessarily improves resilience. Interoperability can create tightly coupled dependencies among equipment from different vendors. Removing or patching one component may disrupt a wider “spider web” of connected systems. Anahi Santiago therefore recommends application rationalization—reducing the number of systems that must be secured—instead of expanding technology sprawl.

Healthcare’s representation in advanced AI-security initiatives is also questioned. The discussion states that no healthcare companies were included among the 50 organizations associated with “Project Glass Wing,” and that healthcare representatives had not yet succeeded in obtaining inclusion. The panel argues that healthcare needs better sector-specific data to justify cybersecurity investment, particularly as margins shrink and security spending competes with clinical capabilities.

A key economic distinction is that the return on investment for security is harder to quantify in healthcare than in financial services. Financial losses from fraud can be calculated directly, whereas the panel notes that healthcare lacks an equivalent widely accepted figure for patient harm, morbidity or mortality resulting from cyber incidents. Hospitals are also described as many businesses operating under one organization, including clinical departments, pharmacies, food services, retail operations, power infrastructure, parking and, in some cases, trauma-related air-traffic functions. This diversity contributes to the complexity of healthcare security.

Finally, the panel discusses beneficial uses of AI in clinical care, particularly medical imaging. Desjardins describes AI systems that can identify subtle indicators of pancreatic cancer on prior CT scans, detect stroke during image acquisition and alert the stroke team before a radiologist completes the interpretation, and prioritize imaging studies containing potentially urgent findings such as pulmonary embolism or new cancer. These examples frame AI as both a significant cyber risk and an important tool for improving clinical outcomes.

## Key technical ideas

1. **Strategic security alignment**
   - Give the CISO a continuing role in enterprise strategy.
   - Plan security capabilities against future clinical models, including hospital-at-home care, virtual care, remote surgery, AI and quantum technologies.

2. **Medical-device and IoT visibility**
   - Maintain a detailed inventory of connected devices.
   - Use device-intake reviews and continuous scanning to identify approved, unknown and rogue assets.
   - Treat medical devices as a distinct and exceptionally large part of the attack surface.

3. **Clinical-engineering and security integration**
   - Embed cybersecurity expertise within clinical engineering.
   - Train clinical-engineering personnel to manage cybersecurity responsibilities alongside device operations.

4. **Segmentation by clinical function**
   - Separate environments such as radiology, pharmacy and laboratory systems where feasible.
   - Progress toward microsegmentation only when communication patterns and legitimate protocols are sufficiently understood.

5. **Legacy-system containment**
   - Recognize that unsupported operating systems and applications may remain clinically essential.
   - Address them through inventory, segmentation, compensating controls and carefully managed replacement or downtime plans.

6. **AI-accelerated offensive operations**
   - AI can enable non-experts to discover weaknesses, generate exploits and conduct attacks.
   - AI may also reverse-engineer vendor patches and produce exploits shortly after patches are released.

7. **Revised vulnerability prioritization**
   - Evaluate combinations of vulnerabilities rather than considering each weakness independently.
   - Reconsider risk models that classify vulnerabilities as low risk when they can be chained into a critical exploit.

8. **Continuous patching and operational resilience**
   - Move beyond fixed patch windows toward faster or continuous patching.
   - Support this with redundant equipment, alternative treatment capacity, rehearsed downtime procedures and rapid recovery processes.

9. **Application rationalization**
   - Reduce unnecessary technology sprawl.
   - A smaller and more controlled application estate may be easier to secure and patch than a larger collection of overlapping systems.

10. **Interoperability as a security dependency**
    - Connected devices from different vendors may use incompatible interfaces and authentication mechanisms.
    - Interoperability can increase systemic coupling, making individual component changes disruptive.

11. **Sector-specific AI-security collaboration**
    - Healthcare needs access to advanced vulnerability-discovery capabilities and better sector data.
    - The panel argues that the unique clinical and operational consequences of healthcare attacks justify stronger healthcare participation in security initiatives.

12. **AI for clinical decision support**
    - Detect subtle disease indicators that may not be obvious to radiologists.
    - Analyze images during acquisition to accelerate emergency notification.
    - Prioritize large imaging backlogs by flagging potentially urgent findings.
