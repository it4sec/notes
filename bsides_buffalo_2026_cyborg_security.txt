## Overview

The presentation examines the security, privacy, safety, and governance implications of connecting medical and recreational devices directly to the human body. Its central argument is that cybernetic devices should be treated simultaneously as medical equipment, software systems, networked computers, and extensions of personal identity.

The talk traces a progression from mechanically controlled pacemakers to software-based and internet-connected implants. As devices become more intelligent and connected, they offer benefits such as battery conservation, automated treatment, telemetry, remote updates, and enhanced human capabilities. However, they also introduce risks involving software defects, insecure communications, proprietary code, centralized infrastructure, data collection, device failure, and limited user control.

The author contrasts two related communities:

- **Medical cybernetics**, which is constrained by extensive regulation, testing, cost, and proprietary technology.
- **Recreational cybernetics**, in which individuals experiment with implants and modifications in lower-regulation environments, often accepting risks that commercial medical-device development would not permit.

Examples in the presentation include pacemakers, artificial heart devices, adaptive and augmentative communication systems, insulin pumps, color-sensing implants, and implanted RFID/NFC chips. These examples are used to show that a failure or compromise is not merely a conventional cybersecurity incident: it can remove a person’s ability to communicate, disrupt essential treatment, expose intimate biological data, or directly threaten life.

A major theme is that users often cannot make informed decisions because the relevant parties—patients, doctors, hardware engineers, software engineers, manufacturers, and regulators—operate in separate silos. The author argues for stronger communication between these groups, greater transparency, more open technical ecosystems, patient-centered risk discussions, and meaningful control over telemetry and device behavior.

# Technical Innovation and Authors’ Ideas

The presentation’s main technical contribution is a practical threat model for “bio-key networks”—systems in which computing devices are attached to, implanted in, or directly integrated with people. Rather than treating these devices only as clinical instruments, the author analyzes the complete system:

1. The implanted or wearable hardware  
2. Embedded and application software  
3. Wireless communication channels  
4. Telemetry collection and storage  
5. Manufacturer or hospital infrastructure  
6. Firmware-update mechanisms  
7. The patient’s physical environment  
8. Legal and organizational controls surrounding the device  

The author’s ideas emphasize that security must be designed around the entire lifecycle of a device, including what happens when it fails, loses connectivity, becomes obsolete, or can no longer be safely removed.

## Key technical ideas

### 1. Medical devices are high-consequence computers

The presentation treats devices such as pacemakers and insulin pumps as software systems whose failures can have physical and potentially life-threatening consequences. As devices evolve from mechanical operation to software-controlled behavior, ordinary software and cybersecurity concerns become directly connected to bodily safety.

The technical implication is that device security cannot be evaluated independently from clinical consequences. A software defect, unauthorized command, failed update, or unavailable server may affect a person’s body rather than merely their data or workstation.

### 2. Unsecured communication channels create unintended control paths

The MiniMed insulin-pump example illustrates how an insecure communication channel intended for a remote controller could be used by other systems. The presentation describes how recreational developers connected the pump to a continuous blood-glucose monitor using bridge code that calculated insulin delivery.

This demonstrates both sides of interoperability:

- An insecure interface can enable beneficial innovation and user-controlled automation.
- The same interface can allow an attacker who knows the device is present to interfere with treatment.

The author therefore presents communication security as a core safety requirement, not simply a privacy feature.

### 3. Open-source systems can enable user-driven innovation

The OpenAPS example is used to show how public code can allow patients and independent developers to create more advanced treatment systems from existing hardware. According to the presentation, derivatives of OpenAPS enabled further development because the original code was publicly available.

The author’s position is not that open source automatically makes a device safe. Instead, open code can:

- Permit inspection for defects
- Give users and clinicians more information
- Enable independent experimentation
- Support innovation outside manufacturer-controlled ecosystems
- Make risk discussions more informed

The presentation summarizes this position as: **“open source bad is better than closed source bad,”** because visible flaws may be more actionable than invisible flaws in proprietary systems.

### 4. Telemetry expands the attack surface and the privacy threat

Internet-connected medical devices introduce a second major system: the telemetry infrastructure. The author questions:

- What information is collected?
- Is all of it necessary?
- Where is it transmitted?
- How is it protected?
- What happens if the receiving server is unavailable?
- Who can correlate it with other personal information?

The presentation argues that biological telemetry can become especially sensitive when combined with names, dates of birth, demographic information, and social-media data. Such correlation could produce a highly detailed profile of an individual.

The proposed technical principle is **data minimization**: collect only what is necessary, avoid unnecessary transmission, securely store information locally where possible, and discard data when it is no longer needed.

### 5. Centralized infrastructure creates systemic failure modes

The talk highlights the risks of devices that depend on manufacturer servers or centralized update systems. A disruption to the server or related corporate infrastructure could affect:

- Device updates
- Medical scheduling
- Shipment of medical equipment
- Treatment-related services
- The ability to monitor or manage devices

The author raises an important design question: if an implanted device cannot contact its server, does it continue operating, fail safely, shut down, or fail in a dangerous way?

This frames **failure behavior** as a critical architectural property. The presentation does not prescribe one universal answer, but argues that these outcomes must be explicitly considered.

### 6. Wireless update functionality involves a difficult risk tradeoff

The discussion of pacemakers presents a conflict between two risks:

- A vulnerability may require a firmware or software update.
- Wireless connectivity may provide an attack path or expose the device to remote interference.

The author describes individuals who chose to disable wireless update functionality because they considered uncontrolled connectivity too risky. This highlights the need for user- and clinician-accessible risk assessments rather than assuming that connectivity is always beneficial.

### 7. Physical security remains relevant for implanted devices

For implanted RFID and NFC chips, the attack surface is physical proximity. The presentation describes the possibility of skimming information when an implanted chip is sufficiently close to a reader.

The proposed mitigation is a physical RFID/NFC-blocking glove with removable layers:

- With the blocking layer in place, scanning is prevented.
- Removing the layer allows the chip to function normally.

This example extends conventional access-control thinking to the human body. The author treats the body itself as part of the security perimeter.

### 8. Device ownership should include repair and modification rights

The presentation connects implanted devices with the right-to-repair movement and raises questions about warranties, software modification, proprietary control, and legal restrictions. The author argues that people may need the right to repair or modify devices that are inside their bodies, while acknowledging that the legal interaction between medical devices, repair rights, and the DMCA is complex.

The technical idea is that a person who depends on a device should not necessarily be locked out of its operation, maintenance, or security evaluation merely because the device is proprietary.

### 9. Security architecture should support local control

The author favors architectures that allow the device user to control how information is collected and where it is stored. One example is storing telemetry on a local SD card rather than automatically uploading it to a remote service.

The recommended controls include:

- Local rather than mandatory cloud storage
- Firewalls and network segmentation
- Minimal telemetry collection
- Explicit data-retention and deletion rules
- Physical defenses for short-range wireless interfaces
- User control over connectivity and updates

### 10. Human and organizational interfaces are security components

A recurring technical and operational idea is that communication gaps between disciplines create security weaknesses. Hardware teams, software teams, doctors, patients, and manufacturers may each understand only part of the overall risk.

The author recommends that developers:

- Build relationships with medical professionals and patients
- Understand clinical needs and patient preferences
- Learn about medical cybersecurity
- Ask whether a proposed feature is genuinely necessary
- Consider the patient’s benefit rather than only the manufacturer’s benefit
- Be prepared to raise concerns or report problems

The presentation also emphasizes that doctors and other medical professionals need better tools and frameworks for discussing cybersecurity, privacy, and ethical tradeoffs with patients.

## Overall assessment

The presentation’s innovation lies primarily in its systems-level framing rather than in introducing a new device or algorithm. It links implant design, wireless security, telemetry, open-source development, physical access control, centralized infrastructure, patient autonomy, and regulation into one unified security problem.

Its strongest technical message is that cybernetic devices should be designed around:

- Minimal data collection
- Transparent and inspectable software where feasible
- Secure communications
- Resilient behavior when networks or servers fail
- Explicit update and deprecation strategies
- Local user control
- Physical protection against proximity attacks
- Patient-centered safety and privacy decisions
- Collaboration across engineering, medicine, and policy

The presentation ultimately advocates for an “informed cyborg”: a user who understands the capabilities, dependencies, data flows, failure modes, and risks of an implanted or wearable system well enough to make an informed decision about adopting, modifying, or rejecting it.
