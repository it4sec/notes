## Summary

The presentation proposes **Internet Exposure Prevention (IEP)**, a secure-by-default capability intended to prevent operational technology (OT) devices from becoming directly accessible from the public internet. Michael Pyle frames the problem as one that cannot be solved reliably through deployment instructions alone. Although vendors commonly advise users not to expose OT devices to the internet, the presentation cites more than **145,000 internet-exposed OT assets globally**, with approximately one-third located in the United States.

The authors’ central technical and operational idea is to embed prevention directly into the device rather than relying on installers or asset owners to configure the system correctly. The solution should permit legitimate outbound communication—such as an OT device communicating with a cloud service—while rejecting unsolicited inbound internet traffic.

Pyle compares the model to telephone behavior: a device may initiate and maintain an expected communication, but it should ignore incoming communication from an unknown source. This approach is intended to make the device effectively invisible to internet scanners and reduce accidental exposure caused by port forwarding, configuration mistakes, unclear instructions, or insufficient operator expertise.

The presentation first develops the approach for IPv4 and IPv6, then addresses an important legacy-networking edge case: customers whose internal networks use publicly routable IP addresses. The proposed adaptations attempt to preserve secure-by-default behavior without assuming that every internal OT network uses private addressing.

A proof of concept compared two otherwise identical devices: one implementing IEP and one without it. In tests conducted both behind a firewall with port forwarding and in a honeypot directly connected to the internet, the unprotected device was discovered by scanners such as **Shodan, Censys, and Criminal IP**. The device implementing IEP did not respond to the probes and did not appear in those services during the reported tests.

The presentation also discusses deployment in existing devices. The concept could be implemented in new products and potentially added to devices already in the field through firmware upgrades. Pyle notes that an alternative rollout strategy could initially detect and alert on exposure, allowing asset owners to decide when to enable blocking. The broader proposal assigns responsibility across vendors, system integrators, and asset owners: vendors provide secure-by-default products, integrators deploy them securely, and asset owners maintain secure operation.

## Key technical ideas

### 1. Secure-by-default prevention at the device level

The primary innovation is to make internet exposure prevention an inherent device behavior rather than an optional deployment practice. The device should protect itself even when installation guidance is misunderstood, ignored, or incorrectly applied.

### 2. Permit expected outbound flows while blocking unsolicited inbound traffic

The solution does not simply block all internet connectivity. It distinguishes between:

- **Outbound or device-initiated communication**, which may be needed for cloud services.
- **Unsolicited inbound communication**, which should be ignored unless it belongs to an already established, device-initiated flow.

This preserves required cloud communication while limiting unsolicited access from the internet.

### 3. IPv4 implementation using address classification

For IPv4, the proposed baseline behavior is:

1. Prefer assigning the device an address from the RFC 1918 private address space.
2. Inspect incoming packets before normal processing.
3. Examine the source IP address.
4. Ignore traffic whose source is publicly routable, unless it is associated with a communication initiated by the device toward a cloud endpoint.
5. Preserve communication from private addresses on the internal network.

### 4. IPv6 implementation using ULA and global-unicast restrictions

For IPv6, the proposal uses:

- **RFC 4193 Unique Local Addresses (ULAs)** as the default addressing model.
- **Global Unicast Addresses (GUAs)** only when the device needs to communicate with the internet.
- A restriction allowing incoming communication associated with the designated prefix used by the customer’s network, while preventing arbitrary internet-originated access.

The presentation argues that even if an attacker spoofs an address from that prefix, the response would be routed back toward the legitimate customer network rather than to the attacker.

### 5. Handling legacy networks with public internal addressing

The authors identify a significant edge case: some legacy customer networks use publicly routable IP addresses internally, including systems established before RFC 1918 private addressing became available.

For such environments, the proposed approach is to:

- Permit assignment of a routable address.
- Require administrative action to enable this mode, potentially through an advanced configuration interface.
- Require the administrator to specify the network’s base address using the first **n bits** of the address.
- Use a lower bound that might be 12 bits, with a proposed default of 16 bits.

This configuration is intended to preserve the prevention mechanism while accommodating internal public addressing. The presentation acknowledges that misconfiguration could cause some leakage or reachability, but argues that the approach retains much of the secure-by-default protection.

### 6. Passive behavior against internet scanners

The proof of concept focuses on making the protected device non-responsive to unsolicited probes. The reported results indicate that:

- The unprotected device responded to scans and was discovered within minutes.
- The protected device generated no effective response to the scanning entities.
- The protected device did not appear in Shodan, Censys, or Criminal IP during the tests.
- The results were observed both behind a firewall with port forwarding and in a directly connected honeypot.

### 7. Applicability to new and deployed devices

The concept is presented as applicable to:

- New OT products designed with IEP from the outset.
- Existing devices updated through firmware.
- Exposure remediation for assets identified through an installed-base program.

The presentation also considers an alert-only deployment mode, in which firmware detects internet access and records or reports it before blocking is enabled.

### 8. Division of security responsibilities

The authors propose a three-part responsibility model:

- **Vendors:** Secure by default and secure by design.
- **System integrators:** Secure deployment and adherence to installation guidance.
- **Asset owners:** Secure operation and maintenance.

This structure is intended to avoid the “I thought somebody else was doing it” problem by making responsibilities explicit.
