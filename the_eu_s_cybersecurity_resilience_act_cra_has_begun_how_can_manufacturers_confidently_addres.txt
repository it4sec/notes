Mars Cheng (TXOne Networks Inc., TW)

The EU’s Cybersecurity Resilience Act (CRA) is a landmark regulation targeting the cybersecurity of products with digital elements within the EU market. While full mandatory enforcement is set for December 2027, manufacturers must comply with critical reporting obligations starting in September 2026. With harmonized standards and third‑party verification bodies (Notified Bodies) still pending clarity, how can manufacturers move from a reactive, rushed state to a proactive, composed compliance strategy?

This presentation will focus on the manufacturer’s reporting obligations and the cybersecurity requirements for “Important Products.” We will share our Product Security Incident Response Team (PSIRT)’s practical approach to planning and evaluating the future compliance roadmap.

## Technical focus

The presentation explains how manufacturers can operationalize compliance with the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), especially:

1. **Reporting obligations**, associated with Article 14 in the presentation.
2. **Manufacturer cybersecurity obligations**, associated with Article 13.
3. **Security requirements for important and critical products.**
4. **Product classification and conformity assessment.**
5. **Cross-functional PSIRT and incident-response preparation.**

The central message is that CRA compliance should not be treated as a last-minute documentation exercise. Manufacturers should begin by building repeatable processes that connect product security, engineering, customer-facing teams, information security, legal, communications, and management.

## Main analysis and summary

### 1. CRA affects products with digital elements

Cheng presents the CRA as unusually broad because it applies to products with digital elements placed on the EU market. The scope therefore extends beyond traditional security products and includes software, firmware, operating systems, networking products, antivirus products, routers, firewalls, VPN solutions, and other connected or digitally enabled products.

The presentation emphasizes that compliance must be addressed across the product lifecycle:

- Design
- Development
- Maintenance
- Vulnerability handling
- Risk assessment
- Security testing
- Technical documentation
- User documentation
- Security update and mitigation processes

The author’s practical interpretation is that manufacturers must prepare product-by-product rather than assuming that an organization-wide security program alone will be sufficient.

### 2. Reporting must be designed for strict time limits

The presentation identifies two reporting-trigger scenarios:

- **Actively exploited vulnerabilities**
- **Serious incidents that affect a manufacturer’s ability to support products**, such as incidents affecting software or firmware updates

The described reporting sequence is time-sensitive:

- **Within 24 hours:** Early-warning notification
- **Within 72 hours:** More detailed vulnerability or incident information, including mitigations where applicable
- **Within 14 days:** Final report for vulnerabilities
- **Within one month:** Final report for incidents

Reports are described as being submitted to the relevant EU reporting or supervisory structures through the CRA single reporting platform, with coordination involving national CSIRTs as stated in the transcript.

A key technical and organizational difficulty is that PSIRT teams may know the affected product and vulnerability but may not know where the product is deployed. Cheng therefore proposes connecting product-security processes with customer-success and sales data, such as records of product versions, customer locations, regions, and deployment information.

### 3. A cross-functional reporting model

Cheng’s proposed approach begins with identifying internal stakeholders. Depending on the scenario, these may include:

- PSIRT or product security
- Research and development
- Quality assurance
- Product management
- Customer success
- CISO or information-security teams
- Legal
- Public relations
- Sales
- Infrastructure and cloud-service teams

The presentation distinguishes between vulnerability cases and serious incidents:

- **Vulnerability cases** primarily require product-security, engineering, product-management, and customer-facing coordination.
- **Serious incidents** may also involve corporate infrastructure, cloud services, public services, and internal information-security teams because those systems could affect product availability, support, or distribution.

The proposed process is:

1. Receive intelligence about a vulnerability or incident.
2. Filter and verify whether the information is credible.
3. Form a task force based on the scenario.
4. Identify affected products, versions, customers, and regions.
5. Prepare the 24-hour early warning.
6. Consolidate technical details and mitigations for the 72-hour notification.
7. Prepare the final report.
8. Notify and coordinate with customers, not only regulators.
9. Review the process through repeated exercises and revise it.

### 4. Tabletop exercises as an engineering and compliance tool

One of the presentation’s strongest practical ideas is the use of repeated tabletop exercises. Cheng reports that conducting exercises revealed more than 32 issues requiring tracking, identification, or revision.

The exercises are intended to test both the written process and its execution under time pressure. Suggested scenarios include:

- Vulnerabilities in products
- Non-vulnerability incidents
- Compromised engineering devices
- Compromised production services
- Incidents affecting update or firmware-delivery capabilities
- Zero-day situations
- Customers using centralized procurement while deploying products across multiple countries

The author treats these discovered problems as valuable findings: identifying operational gaps before a real incident allows the manufacturer to improve procedures, clarify responsibilities, and establish evidence of preparedness.

### 5. Product-location visibility is a major operational problem

A recurring issue is the gap between product-security knowledge and deployment knowledge. A manufacturer may know which product and version are vulnerable but lack reliable information about:

- Where the product is actually deployed
- Which countries are affected
- Whether the customer resold or redistributed the product
- Whether centralized procurement obscures final deployment locations

Cheng suggests leveraging customer-success systems and other cloud-based business records to improve this visibility. The technical idea is not merely to maintain vulnerability data, but to correlate vulnerability intelligence with product inventory, customer information, versions, regions, and deployment context.

### 6. Classification and conformity assessment

For manufacturer obligations, Cheng describes a classification-driven process. Products are grouped into categories such as:

- Default products
- Important Class I products
- Important Class II products
- Critical products

The appropriate conformity-assessment route depends on the product category and its associated security requirements. The presentation mentions several conformity-assessment modules:

- **Module A:** Described as allowing manufacturer self-assessment or self-declaration.
- **Module B plus C:** Described as involving third-party verification.
- **Module H:** Presented as a quality-system-oriented route that may be more suitable for very large organizations with extensive product lines.

The suggested sequence is:

1. Identify the product category.
2. Determine the applicable security requirements and standards.
3. Perform a product-specific gap assessment.
4. Select an appropriate conformity-assessment route.
5. Establish or improve the security processes.
6. Produce technical documentation and user documentation.
7. Conduct testing and collect evidence.
8. Complete the declaration of conformity.

Cheng notes that organizations with many products may need to repeat this process across individual product lines. He also states that free or unpaid software is not exempt when it is placed on the EU market, according to his interpretation of the CRA.

### 7. Gap analysis covers the full product-security program

The gap analysis is described as covering much more than vulnerability scanning. It includes:

- Secure-by-default principles
- Product-security lifecycle processes
- Vulnerability handling
- Incident handling
- Risk assessment
- Security testing
- Technical documentation
- User manuals
- Evidence that required controls and processes operate effectively

The proposed implementation path moves from simpler controls to more comprehensive ones:

- Security checklists
- Threat modeling and risk assessment
- Security requirements analysis
- Vulnerability-handling procedures
- Security testing
- Documentation and evidence collection

Cheng also emphasizes monitoring the evolving standards because the detailed harmonized standards and related requirements were described as being published in stages.

## Technical innovation and authors’ ideas

The presentation does not introduce a new algorithm, protocol, or software system. Its principal innovation is a **practical operating model for turning CRA requirements into an executable product-security and incident-response capability**.

The main ideas are:

1. **Treat regulatory reporting as an operational system, not a compliance document.**  
   The 24-hour, 72-hour, and final-report deadlines require predefined roles, escalation routes, information sources, and decision criteria.

2. **Connect PSIRT intelligence with business and deployment data.**  
   Vulnerability response must be able to identify affected products, versions, customers, and geographic regions. Customer-success and sales systems are therefore treated as important security data sources.

3. **Use scenario-based, cross-functional tabletop exercises.**  
   Exercises expose execution failures that are difficult to identify through policy review alone. Repetition is used to refine procedures and create a more reliable response capability.

4. **Separate vulnerability workflows from serious-incident workflows.**  
   The two scenarios involve different technical evidence, internal stakeholders, infrastructure dependencies, and customer impacts.

5. **Include customer notification as part of responsible response.**  
   Regulatory reporting is presented as only the first layer. Manufacturers must also communicate with customers to manage operational impact and protect product and company reputation.

6. **Use product classification to drive compliance planning.**  
   The product category determines the relevant security requirements and conformity-assessment approach. This makes classification an early architectural and project-planning decision.

7. **Build product-security visibility and threat intelligence capabilities.**  
   Cheng recommends not only responding to incoming intelligence but also developing the ability to hunt for vulnerabilities affecting products “in the wild.”

8. **Plan for evidence-based conformity.**  
   Compliance requires more than having controls in place. Manufacturers must be able to demonstrate lifecycle processes, testing, risk assessments, vulnerability handling, documentation, and mitigations.

## Key technical ideas

- CRA reporting is triggered by actively exploited vulnerabilities and serious incidents affecting product support or operation.
- Reporting workflows should be engineered around strict deadlines: 24 hours, 72 hours, and final reports within 14 days or one month depending on the scenario.
- A CRA response team must include more than PSIRT: engineering, QA, product management, customer success, sales, CISO, infrastructure, legal, and PR may all be required.
- Product-security teams need access to reliable product, version, customer, region, and deployment information.
- Centralized procurement creates a product-location visibility problem that requires additional tracking mechanisms.
- Repeated tabletop exercises are essential for validating real execution capability.
- Vulnerability and serious-incident response should use separate but coordinated workflows.
- Product categories—default, important, and critical—determine security requirements and conformity-assessment paths.
- Gap analysis should cover secure-by-default design, lifecycle security, threat modeling, risk assessment, vulnerability handling, incident response, testing, and documentation.
- Manufacturers should monitor the staged publication of CRA standards and identify the requirements applicable to each product type.
- Compliance should be prepared before the final enforcement deadline because documentation, testing, review, remediation, and third-party assessment may require substantial time.
- The presentation’s main contribution is an actionable compliance roadmap based on stakeholder coordination, product visibility, exercises, gap assessment, and evidence production.
