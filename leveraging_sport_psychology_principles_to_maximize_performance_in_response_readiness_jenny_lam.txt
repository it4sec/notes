## Key technical ideas

### 1. Focus on the response process

In a major incident, analysts may become distracted by questions such as whether the breach is worsening, whether the CISO or manager will be angry, or whether the organization is “winning” or “losing.” These thoughts consume attention but are largely outside the individual’s control.

The recommended response is to return attention to:

- The current investigation task.
- The applicable procedure.
- The next containment or analysis step.
- Documentation and evidence capture.
- Regular communication.
- Alternative investigative hypotheses when the current path is unproductive.

The principle is summarized as **controlling the controllable**.

### 2. Use frustration as a diagnostic signal

Frustration is presented as a possible sign that the responder has shifted from process-focused reasoning to outcome-focused thinking. In practical terms, this should prompt the analyst to step back and ask:

- What task am I actually responsible for now?
- Which investigative techniques have already been tried?
- What search parameters or hypotheses have not been considered?
- What does the documented process require next?

This creates a simple mental reset for high-pressure technical investigations.

### 3. Build confidence through repeated action

The confidence cycle consists of:

1. Reframing negative self-assessment into a more tangible and constructive view.
2. Taking a manageable risk or stepping outside one’s comfort zone.
3. Experiencing success, or at least making progress.
4. Recognizing and reinforcing the achievement.
5. Gradually expanding the individual’s confidence and willingness to act.

In a SOC, this can mean encouraging an uncertain analyst to share an unusual observation. Even if the observation is ultimately a false positive, communicating it may help rule something out and confirms that the analyst’s contribution is valid.

### 4. Make it safe to speak up

Incident response requires information to move quickly across the team. The talk highlights the danger of analysts withholding observations because they fear interrupting senior staff or being wrong.

A response team should therefore create an environment where:

- Uncertain but relevant findings can be raised.
- Contributions are acknowledged.
- False positives are treated as useful information when they help narrow the investigation.
- Team members are encouraged to contribute regardless of personality or perceived technical status.

### 5. Build a balanced and resilient response team

A response team should not depend on a small number of individuals or a single type of expertise. The author compares this to a sports team in which different players have different positions and responsibilities.

Operational implications include:

- Defining roles clearly.
- Balancing technical and wider response capabilities.
- Maintaining backup capability when key people are unavailable.
- Avoiding excessive dependency on specific individuals.
- Ensuring the team has sufficient depth to sustain response activity.

### 6. Establish mission and culture

The author uses the example of the NASA cleaner who describes their work as helping build a spaceship to illustrate how a shared mission can connect every role to the wider objective.

For cybersecurity teams, a shared mission helps personnel understand why their work matters—for example, protecting an organization, its industry, or vulnerable people from attackers. Leaders are expected to reinforce this mission through their own behavior and by encouraging the desired team culture.

### 7. Treat exercises as performance-development activities

Exercises should test more than whether a documented process exists. They can also be used to develop:

- Individual confidence.
- Communication under pressure.
- Willingness to take action.
- Team cohesion.
- Psychological safety.
- Leadership and role clarity.
- The organization’s broader response culture.

The author distinguishes between having basic technical skills and using performance principles to improve how effectively those skills are applied during stressful situations.
